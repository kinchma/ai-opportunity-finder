# Laurel Run Trailer Works — Order-to-Delivery Interview

**Fictional simulation:** This is an imagined interview for an academic case, not a transcript of an actual conversation. Laurel Run, its people, dealers, records and events are fictional.

**Interviewee:** Priya Ellwood, Dealer Account Coordinator  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Dealer order to delivery  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes, including natural pauses

[00:00]

**Interviewer:** Thanks for talking with me, Priya. Could you start with where you fit in the business and what an order looks like from your side?

**SME:** I'm the Dealer Account Coordinator, and I report to Tessa Bellamy, our Dealer Sales Manager. I handle dealer requests and routine quote templates, make the dealer order after written acceptance, and answer questions about where an order stands. I work with sales and customer service, but I don't run the production floor or approve my own quotes.

**Interviewer:** Who is the customer in that conversation?

**SME:** An independent dealer. We don't sell directly to retail customers. The dealer sets the retail price, so the amount on a Laurel Run quote is a dealer amount, not something I can present as the customer's retail price. Dealers arrange or accept delivery by appointment.

**Interviewer:** How does a request reach you, and what do you need before you can prepare a quote?

**SME:** A request can come through Outlook or by telephone. Holly Darnell at reception routes calls and takes handwritten messages for me. I check the dealer ID against our working Excel dealer list and the quote records. For a new quote I capture the dealer ID, the model, dimensions, options and quantity in the Excel template. Those details matter because a similar model name doesn't tell the floor which options were requested.

**Interviewer:** Do you decide the price as you go?

**SME:** I complete the routine template. Tessa maintains the formula and rate workbook and approves the quote; exceptional pricing or formula questions go to her. I can describe what goes into the template, but I wouldn't claim to own the formula logic. Until the dealer accepts, the quote is an offer, not an order.

**Interviewer:** And where do payment terms come in?

**SME:** I check the recorded credit status before applying terms. Net 30 from the shipment invoice is standard for a dealer approved for credit. An active account isn't, by itself, evidence of a credit approval. If a status says “not specified,” that doesn't establish either approval or denial. I'm not sure from these records who decides a particular unresolved credit question, so I wouldn't turn that blank into an answer.

**Interviewer:** What makes the transition from an offer to an order clear?

**SME:** The dealer's written acceptance. We keep that with the email in Outlook. Once it is accepted in writing, I make the dealer order in the Excel order register. A phone inquiry, or a quote we've prepared, isn't the same thing as that acceptance.

[04:00]

**Interviewer:** Could we use a specific example to see how the records fit together?

**SME:** Yes. Cedar Bend Trailer Sales is dealer DLR-0001. The list shows it as active and approved for credit. On October 14, 2026, it requested quote QTE-2026-1180 for one 6-by-12 Trailfield. The dealer base was $4,800, and the selected option was a spare-tire mount only, at $120. The total was $4,920. I prepared the quote and Tessa approved it.

**Interviewer:** “Mount only” seems worth pausing on. What does that tell you?

**SME:** It tells me not to expand the option into something else when I'm transferring the information. The record says spare-tire mount only; it doesn't say a spare tire was included. I'd carry the model and the option as approved, rather than adding a tire because the word “spare” appears. And the $4,920 is the dealer quote, not a retail sale price.

**Interviewer:** What happened after the quote?

**SME:** Cedar Bend accepted in writing on October 15. Dealer order ORD-2026-1120 and work order WO-2026-1240 are dated October 15 as well, and Jonah Sutter, our Production Scheduler, scheduled the work order. Those are different identifiers for different records. The quote ID doesn't become the order ID, and the order ID isn't the work-order ID.

**Interviewer:** What exactly do you do to get the accepted details to production?

**SME:** I re-key the approved model and options into the Excel order register and the paper work-order build packet. The written acceptance stays with the Outlook email. So when I look back at the information, the email, register and paper packet each have a part to play. The order register is my lookup for the dealer order; the packet carries the build information to the floor.

**Interviewer:** If the dealer ordered several trailers together, would that still be one work order?

**SME:** Not under the process. One dealer order can cover several trailers, but Jonah assigns a unique work order to each trailer and gives it a target date. Cedar Bend's example is simpler: the quantity was one, and the example has one work order. I wouldn't infer a vehicle identification number from that work-order number; the sample doesn't provide a VIN.

**Interviewer:** Is the date Jonah assigns a commitment you can make to the dealer?

**SME:** It's a target, an estimate rather than a guarantee. I'm not sure what target date was assigned to Cedar Bend's unit; the example doesn't give it. We do know its finish inspection and shipment dates, but those aren't a substitute for a recorded original target.

[08:00]

**Interviewer:** What does Jonah do with the work after it's ready to be scheduled?

**SME:** Each week he checks approved orders, stock notes and supervisor input. He sequences the work orders in his Excel schedule and on the board, then gives the paper packets to the floor. Theo Lowry and the fabrication crew fabricate the trailer; the packet goes through paint and on to Avery Carver and the assembly crew. That's my understanding of the handoff, though Jonah and the supervisors would know more about the detailed floor sequence than I do.

**Interviewer:** How does information come back to you if a dealer wants an update?

**SME:** Theo and Avery report progress and material exceptions to Jonah, and he updates the schedule. If a target moves, he tells me so I can update the dealer. If a dealer asks me for status, I check the order register and ask Jonah or Mateo Renshaw in shipping for the current position before replying. The register helps me identify the order, but I don't treat a previous target as proof of where the trailer is today.

**Interviewer:** What might you have to put together for a useful answer?

**SME:** [Pauses.] First, which dealer and which order they're asking about. Then I need the current position from the person who has it. Jonah is the production scheduler; Mateo coordinates shipping. Depending on where the trailer is, I may need one of them to clarify it. A realistic answer could distinguish a scheduled target from a confirmed shipment. I'm not sure the sample gives a typical response time for those checks, so I can't put a number on it.

**Interviewer:** Do you work out of a single system that shows all of that?

**SME:** No. The working dealer list and order register are in Excel, dealer communications and acceptances are in Outlook, Jonah has an Excel schedule and a board, and the work packet is paper. We have a Zoho CRM license for three users, and I've entered a small pilot set of contacts, but the dealer contact spreadsheet and email remain the working record. I wouldn't describe that pilot as a complete live order-status system.

**Interviewer:** How often do dealers follow up?

**SME:** The case year has about 300 dealer status follow-ups, handled separately from stockout inquiries and warranty cases. That's a count of follow-ups, not a count of late orders or distinct dealers. It doesn't tell me how long each answer took, whether the dealer called more than once about the same unit, or how many targets were missed. I'm not sure from these records.

**Interviewer:** Does a material exception automatically mean a substitute can be used?

**SME:** No. I wouldn't promise that to a dealer. Felix Harrow, our Manufacturing Engineer, and Iris Calder, Quality and Warranty Lead, need to confirm fit and required specifications before a material substitution. Brakes, tires, couplers, lighting and substitutions need fit and applicable compliance review. A component rating by itself doesn't establish the completed trailer's payload; the finished unit's certification and load labels give its GVWR and payload.

[12:00]

**Interviewer:** Suppose a dealer wants to change a specification after accepting an order. What is the normal handoff?

**SME:** Tessa gets the dealer's approval for the specification change. Jonah issues a dated replacement packet page. Theo and Avery return the old page, and Iris checks the current revision. I'm describing the process, not saying Cedar Bend made a change; its example doesn't record one. The approval and the page used on the floor both matter because a request alone isn't the approved build specification.

**Interviewer:** What is it like to keep that straight when a packet is already moving?

**SME:** [Pauses.] Well, the paper has to move among people doing different parts of the work, and the change has a replacement page. There are 24 examples a year where a changed page is checked against an older copy. That tells you something about the checking work, but not that 24 trailers were built incorrectly. I'm not sure how many discrepancies those checks found; the process record doesn't say.

**Interviewer:** Would you yourself approve the technical change or a substitution so the dealer gets an answer sooner?

**SME:** No. Tessa gets dealer approval for the specification change, and Felix and Iris have the fit and required-specification confirmation for substitution. I can relay the order and dealer information, but I don't have their authority or their technical knowledge. If I don't know the effect on a target, I need Jonah's current information rather than giving a made-up date.

**Interviewer:** Once the unit is built, what happens before it can be shipped?

**SME:** Iris and the finish staff check and sign the final inspection. The paper inspection checklist stays with the work-order packet. An inspection concern should be flagged to Iris before shipping. I'm not the person signing the inspection, and I can't tell you from this sample every check performed on Cedar Bend's trailer.

**Interviewer:** What does the example actually establish?

**SME:** It records Cedar Bend's finish inspection on October 26, 2026, followed by shipment on October 27. It doesn't give the inspection findings or an original target date. It also doesn't document a later dealer delivery or retail sale date. The shipping step is important, but I shouldn't fill in events just because they might usually happen around it.

[16:00]

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the shipping and accounting handoff as far as you know it.

**SME:** Mateo schedules shipment, notes delivery and sends shipping confirmation to Elena Corwin, our Accounting Clerk. Elena issues the dealer invoice in QuickBooks Desktop Enterprise at shipment. Under our fictional sales terms, shipment transfers control; accepting an order doesn't yet make it revenue. I'm on the dealer side of that handoff, so Elena or Mateo would be better placed to explain their detailed records.

**Interviewer:** And for Cedar Bend?

**SME:** Shipment and invoice INV-2026-1161 are both dated October 27, for $4,920. The terms are net 30 from the shipment invoice. There was no separate freight charge in the case year, and sales tax is excluded from the amount shown. The invoice amount matches the base and the one stated option; it isn't a retail amount or evidence of a later dealer sale.

**Interviewer:** When you say Mateo notes delivery, can you say when Cedar Bend received this particular unit?

**SME:** I'm not sure. The general process says Mateo notes delivery, but the example gives a shipment date, not a separate delivery date. Dealers arrange or accept delivery by appointment, and I wouldn't use the invoice date to assert when the dealer received it. Those are different facts.

**Interviewer:** Looking across the sequence, what gives you confidence that you're talking about the right trailer?

**SME:** I start with the dealer and the distinct identifiers: the quote, the dealer order and the work order. For Cedar Bend there is only one unit in the example, but a multi-trailer order would have a work order for each trailer. I also need the approved configuration, not just a familiar dealer name. Then I check the relevant current position with Jonah or Mateo instead of assuming a status from the order's existence.

**Interviewer:** Where can the work feel less straightforward from your desk?

**SME:** There can be a pause while I check the register and ask for the current position. The accepted details are in the email, the order is in a register, and a paper packet goes through the shop. When the dealer asks a specific question, I have to make sure I'm using information that corresponds to the right order and is current. I can see those handoffs; I can't infer from the sample exactly how much time each one takes.

**Interviewer:** Are there other measures you'd want before drawing conclusions about performance?

**SME:** I'd want to know the original and revised targets alongside actual shipment dates, and how often those targets changed. I'd also want response times for status questions and the reasons for changes, with the scope of each measure made clear. The example is one linked order, not a full order ledger. The 300 follow-ups don't by themselves tell us an on-time rate or a cause of delay, and a missing measurement isn't zero.

**Interviewer:** Anything else we should keep in mind about the scale of the work?

**SME:** The case year has 1,500 trailers completed and shipped, with one work order per trailer; roughly 30 were completed per working week over 50 working weeks. Cedar Bend's unit is included in October's 50 Trailfield shipments. Those counts give context, but they don't say how many dealer orders there were, since an order can cover more than one trailer.

**Interviewer:** Thank you. We're at about twenty minutes. That distinction between what your records show and what you'd need to ask someone else is helpful.

**SME:** You're welcome. I'd rather check the current position than make the dealer a promise I can't support.
