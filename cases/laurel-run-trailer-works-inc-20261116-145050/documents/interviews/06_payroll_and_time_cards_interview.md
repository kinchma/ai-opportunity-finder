# Laurel Run Trailer Works — Payroll and time cards

**Fictional simulation:** This is a constructed one-on-one interview, not a record of an actual conversation. Laurel Run Trailer Works and the people and records described are fictional.

**Interviewee:** Nia Hadley, Office and Payroll Administrator  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Payroll and time-card collection, review and submission  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes, including natural pauses; timestamps are approximate.

### [00:00]

**Interviewer:** Nia, could you start with your part of payroll and the point at which it becomes your work? I’m interested in the actual sequence, not just the name of the system.

**SME:** I’m the Office and Payroll Administrator, and I report to Lydia Fenner, our Controller. I collect the time information and prepare payroll. The period is 14 days, but I think about it as two separate Sunday-through-Saturday workweeks. We have 26 biweekly periods in a year. People record their time or exceptions, the hourly cards go to the appropriate approvers, and then the approved cards come to me. I prepare the weekly amounts, enter them, check them against the cards, review totals with Lydia and submit payroll.

**Interviewer:** What do people put on the card before it ever reaches you?

**SME:** The hourly employees record start and end times, breaks and weekly totals on paper cards. There are 36 hourly employees in the snapshot. Thirty-two are shop employees, and that includes our two working supervisors; the other four are in office, receiving and shipping roles. For the 14 salaried employees, the routine uses paper exception notices rather than hourly cards.

**Interviewer:** What does an exception notice tell you compared with the card?

**SME:** It’s the paper notice used for a salaried employee’s exception. I’m not sure of the full set of circumstances or the detailed fields on that notice from these records. I wouldn’t turn the illustrative salaried hours in the sample into time-card entries or assume that a blank regular-hours field is an exception notice. They’re different records for different purposes.

**Interviewer:** Are those categories the same thing as a legal overtime classification?

**SME:** No. “Hourly” and “salary” describe the pay basis in this roster; pay basis alone doesn’t establish someone’s legal overtime classification. The sample leaves the salaried regular and overtime fields blank, and that blank doesn’t answer the classification question either. If you ask me for a determination on an individual beyond the information here, I’m not sure; I wouldn’t make one from the shape of the spreadsheet.

**Interviewer:** And your own workload—does payroll occupy your whole job?

**SME:** No. The role matrix estimates my time at about 80 percent HR and payroll, 15 percent finance and accounting, and 5 percent facilities and administration. Those are approximate shares, not separate jobs. Payroll is an important part of the work, but it isn’t everything I do.

### [04:00]

**Interviewer:** Take me through the approval handoffs. Who has to see the cards before they reach you?

**SME:** Theo Lowry, the Fabrication Supervisor, signs for his direct reports, and Avery Carver, the Assembly Supervisor, signs for hers. They both work with their crews and have their own hourly cards, too. For the sample period, their manager, Plant Manager Owen Bellamy, is the planned approver of those two cards. I want to be precise there: that’s the planned routing for the sample, not evidence that he has already signed them.

**Interviewer:** What about the hourly people who aren’t on those crews?

**SME:** Lydia is the relevant approver for Elena Corwin, the Accounting Clerk. Mara Bellamy, our President and CEO, is the approver for Holly Darnell, Reception and Admin Coordinator. Devon Kersey, Materials and Purchasing Manager, is the approver for Cora Whitlock in receiving and inventory and Mateo Renshaw in shipping. After the appropriate approval, the cards come to me.

**Interviewer:** So if I saw a supervisor’s name on a card, that would mean the supervisor is the employee, the approver, or both?

**SME:** It depends on whose card you’re looking at. Theo signs for his direct reports; that’s different from the card for Theo’s own hours. Likewise, Avery signs for her direct reports, but she has her own card. For those supervisors’ cards in the sample, Owen is the planned approver. I wouldn’t assume that a person signs their own hours just because they sign for a crew.

**Interviewer:** Suppose you get a card and a time or signature is unclear. How do you handle it?

**SME:** I check with the employee and the relevant approver. It might be a start or end time, a break, a weekly total or a signature that’s missing or unclear. The corrected card goes back for the appropriate signoff. I can’t just fill in an hour because it looks likely, and a card that hasn’t arrived isn’t zero hours. If it cannot be resolved, I bring it promptly to Lydia for direction.

**Interviewer:** Is that a description of a specific problem in the October sample?

**SME:** No, I’m describing the procedure if that happens. I’m not sure how many cards needed correction in that period; the summary doesn’t say. The practical part is that a question can send you back to both the employee and the approver, and then the corrected card needs signoff. Until the question is settled, I don’t have a clean approved figure to treat as established.

**Interviewer:** If you’re away, who takes over submission?

**SME:** I’m not sure. The procedure says to bring an unresolved case, or my absence, promptly to Lydia for direction. It doesn’t name a backup payroll submitter or an alternate card approver. I wouldn’t substitute Lydia’s direction for an invented assignment of those roles.

### [08:00]

**Interviewer:** Once you have the approved information, what do you work with on your desk and on the computer?

**SME:** The office roster and Excel payroll preparation are part of my work. I collect the approved paper cards and paper exception notices, and I enter the approved weekly regular and overtime amounts into RUN Powered by ADP. Then I check the entries and totals against the cards. Lydia and I review the payroll totals before I submit payroll. The approved paper cards and exception notices are kept with the payroll preparation records for review, and they’re handled securely.

**Interviewer:** What is the point of keeping the weekly figures separate when the pay period is two weeks?

**SME:** The two-week total alone doesn’t show the regular and overtime amounts for each workweek. You need to keep the Sunday-to-Saturday boundaries in view. Micah Orlin’s illustrative period is a good example: he’s a Fabricator/Welder on Theo’s crew. The sample covers Sunday, October 18 through Saturday, October 31, 2026. In the first week, October 18 through 24, it shows 40 hours: 40 regular and no overtime. In the second, October 25 through 31, it shows 42: 40 regular and two overtime. The two weeks total 82 hours.

**Interviewer:** If you were looking only at “82,” what would you lose?

**SME:** I wouldn’t see which week had the two overtime hours, or the approved weekly breakdown I need to enter. The paper card has the weekly information, and I check the payroll entries and totals against it. The period total helps as a check, but it doesn’t replace the two separate weekly figures. The sample is a summary of an illustrative paper card, not the entire annual payroll ledger.

**Interviewer:** Can you walk through the dollar example without implying it applies to everyone?

**SME:** Yes. The sample gives Micah a $24 hourly rate and a $36 overtime rate for this example. Eighty regular hours at $24, plus two overtime hours at $36, gives $1,992 gross before withholding. That’s a fictional example, not a general legal interpretation or a statement of anybody’s net pay. The other employees’ blank rates and gross amounts in the sample aren’t zero; they simply aren’t supplied there.

**Interviewer:** Are we looking at an already approved card and an already paid amount?

**SME:** No. As of the October 31 snapshot, Theo’s approval of Micah’s card and the other hourly supervisory approvals are planned for Monday, November 2. Payday is planned for November 5. Neither the approvals nor payday are recorded as completed at the snapshot. So I can explain what the example shows and what is planned, but I can’t tell you those future steps happened.

### [12:00]

**Interviewer:** Could we look at another pattern in the same summary, so the weekly split isn’t just a rule you’re repeating?

**SME:** Sure. Daphne Weller, another Fabricator/Welder, has 38 hours in the first week and 40 in the second, for 78 total in the illustrative sample. The weekly regular amounts are 38 and 40, with zero overtime shown in each week. Theo is her planned approver for the sample. The data don’t tell me why her first week is 38, so I wouldn’t attach a reason to it.

**Interviewer:** And someone outside Theo’s crew?

**SME:** Cora Whitlock’s sample shows 40 hours in the first week and 41 in the second: 40 regular and one overtime hour in that second week, 81 hours altogether. Devon is the planned approver of her card. That gives you the same need to preserve the weekly breakdown, but a different manager in the handoff. The sample doesn’t provide Cora’s rate or gross pay, so I can’t calculate her pay from it.

**Interviewer:** Let’s say the total on a card doesn’t match the time and breaks. Do you correct the spreadsheet and keep going?

**SME:** Not on my own assumption. [Pause.] I check with the employee and the relevant approver to resolve the discrepancy, and the corrected card goes back for the appropriate signoff. The issue could be the total, but it could also be one of the times or a break. Until we know which, changing a number to make the arithmetic look tidy could put the wrong number into payroll. If it can’t be resolved, Lydia needs to give direction promptly.

**Interviewer:** Does Lydia’s review of the payroll totals replace the individual manager’s card approval?

**SME:** No. Those are separate steps in the sequence. The appropriate manager signs the card; I enter the approved weekly amounts and check the entries and totals against the cards; then Lydia and I review payroll totals before I submit. The records don’t describe every detail of Lydia’s review, so I’m not sure what additional checks she may perform. I can explain our stated handoff without claiming that one signoff stands in for another.

**Interviewer:** How much time is allowed for your part?

**SME:** About two hours per period for my hourly-card entry and verification. That’s an estimate for that portion, not a measured duration for every card, and it doesn’t include the supervisors’ separate review and signoff. I’m not sure how much total time the managers spend or how long a particular correction takes. The card has to go through the people who can clarify and approve it; the sample doesn’t measure that waiting time.

### [16:00]

**Interviewer:** What would you want a reader of the sample file to understand before using it to describe the whole payroll operation?

**SME:** First, it covers all 50 staff IDs for one illustrative biweekly period, not a year of payroll transactions. Hourly rows are paper-card summaries, while salaried weekly hours are illustrative estimates, not hourly cards. Second, the blank salary regular and overtime fields and the blank pay amounts for most people aren’t evidence of zero. And third, a planned approval date is not a completed approval. Those distinctions matter when the file is passed around as if it were a ledger.

**Interviewer:** How would you know whether this process is getting easier or harder across periods? What measures do you have here?

**SME:** We have the approximate two hours for my entry and verification and the number of hourly employees in the snapshot. I’m not sure how many cards come back with questions over a year, how often approvals are delayed, or how long each handoff takes. The illustrative file doesn’t supply those measures, and I wouldn’t infer them from its weekly totals. It also doesn’t tell us what a complete year of payroll errors or corrections looked like.

**Interviewer:** What is the everyday point of friction for you, without assuming a particular correction actually happened in the sample?

**SME:** Getting a question answered by the right people and getting a corrected card signed can take attention. The employee knows their time; the relevant approver has the signoff role. I need the approved weekly figures to enter, and the card is also what I check against afterward. I can be ready to do the entry, but if a time or signature isn’t clear, the next useful step is a conversation and a return for signoff, not a guess.

**Interviewer:** Once Lydia and you have reviewed the totals, where does your responsibility stop, based on what we have?

**SME:** The stated sequence is that I submit payroll after that review and keep the approved paper cards and exception notices with the payroll preparation records for review, handling them securely. I’m not sure of a more detailed retention schedule, storage location or post-submission correction procedure from these records. I wouldn’t make those up or suggest the planned November payday already occurred.

**Interviewer:** Last question: if the planned approvals can’t be obtained or you’re unavailable, what should someone take away?

**SME:** Bring the unresolved situation, or my absence, promptly to Lydia for direction. Don’t turn a missing card into zero, and don’t assume another named person has authority to submit or approve just because their name is on the organization chart. We know the normal manager routes and the sample’s planned supervisor-card approver. Beyond that, the records don’t name an alternate.

**Interviewer:** That makes sense. Thank you for separating what the example shows from what is still planned.

**SME:** You’re welcome. That distinction is important when someone reads the period summary after the fact.

*Closing: approximately 20:00.*
