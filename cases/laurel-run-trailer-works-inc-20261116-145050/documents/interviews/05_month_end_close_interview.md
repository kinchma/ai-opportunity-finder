# Month-end close — simulated interview

**Fictional case disclosure:** This is a simulated, text-only interview for Laurel Run Trailer Works, Inc.; neither the company nor the interview occurred in the real world.

**Interviewee:** Lydia Fenner, Controller  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Month-end close  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes; timestamps are approximate.

## [00:00]

**Interviewer:** Lydia, could you start by describing what month-end close means in your job? I’m interested in the work as it actually moves between people, rather than a textbook definition.

**SME:** I own the monthly close and the paper checklist. Elena Corwin, our Accounting Clerk, handles the dealer invoices and supplier bills. I review the bank reconciliation, receivables and payables reports, compare shipping with invoices, and prepare the management summary from selected reports. I also review and record approved adjustments as applicable. After my review, I sign the checklist. We keep the checklist along with the supporting accounting, shipping and report records for that period.

**Interviewer:** What starts the process, and what does a good finish look like?

**SME:** We do it every month around the month-end cutoff. The aim is to finish within five business days after month-end. That is a target, not a promise that each close has been finished by then. A completed checklist, with the steps initialed and my signoff after review, is the evidence shown for a completed close. It’s not a substitute for the supporting records.

**Interviewer:** If you’re away, does someone else have authority to sign?

**SME:** I’m not sure who would act as an alternate; the records don’t name one. The instruction I can stand behind is to leave my review and signoff pending if I’m away. I wouldn’t call the close signed simply because the other work had been done.

**Interviewer:** How does the close sit within the organization?

**SME:** I report to Mara Bellamy, our President and CEO. Elena and Nia Hadley report to me. For this conversation, Elena’s cutoff work is the immediate connection. Shipping and receiving are in the materials and purchasing organization, so getting the source detail involves people outside my direct reporting line. The accounting records themselves are in QuickBooks Desktop Enterprise. Excel is used for exported reports; there are paper records as well.

**Interviewer:** Are you covering every financial process when you say “close”?

**SME:** No. I have other responsibilities, including reviewing payroll totals with Nia, but I would keep that distinct from this close walkthrough. The approximate time I’ll mention for the report work doesn’t include payroll entry or Elena’s individual bill-packet matching. [Pause.] Those boundaries matter if you’re trying to understand what a number represents.

## [04:00]

**Interviewer:** Let’s start at the cutoff. What has to happen on the shipping side before you can compare records?

**SME:** Mateo Renshaw, our Shipping Coordinator, notes the shipment and sends the shipping confirmation to Elena. Elena issues the dealer invoice in QuickBooks at shipment and posts shipment invoices through the accounting cutoff. When I review, I compare the shipping records with the dealer invoice records for that period. It’s a comparison of records, not just a count of orders received.

**Interviewer:** Why is the distinction between an order and a shipment important here?

**SME:** Under our fictional sales terms, control transfers at shipment. An accepted order by itself isn’t revenue. For approved-credit dealers, net 30 runs from the shipment invoice. Amounts collected as sales tax are excluded from revenue, and no freight was separately charged in this reporting year. I would want to know which period the actual shipment belongs to before treating the invoice as that period’s shipment revenue.

**Interviewer:** Can you use a specific example without turning it into the entire month’s result?

**SME:** Cedar Bend Trailer Sales, dealer DLR-0001, is an example. The order is ORD-2026-1120, and the separate work order is WO-2026-1240. It was for one Trailfield with a spare-tire mount only. The shipment and invoice INV-2026-1161 are dated October 27, 2026. The invoice is $4,920 with net-30 terms. The order, work order and invoice are distinct records; I wouldn’t assume their identifiers mean the same thing just because they relate to one unit.

**Interviewer:** What would you do with that example in your review?

**SME:** I would compare the shipment and invoice records for the relevant period, using the underlying detail if something didn’t line up. If there were a missing shipping detail, I could ask Mateo; for invoice detail, Elena. That’s the procedure, not a claim that we found a discrepancy on the Cedar Bend shipment. An unresolved item stays open for review rather than being marked complete.

**Interviewer:** Could the monthly shipment log tell you October revenue by itself?

**SME:** No. It shows units. The Cedar Bend unit is already included in the 50 October Trailfield shipments. The log also shows 45 Enclosure and 30 Workspan shipments, for 125 trailers in all. It isn’t a complete revenue ledger for options or replacement parts. Even though the example has a dollar amount, I wouldn’t use that one invoice as though it priced every Trailfield in the log, or add its unit to the 125 again.

## [08:00]

**Interviewer:** Let’s turn to the supplier side. What does Elena need before a bill is entered, and how does that connect to your close?

**SME:** Elena posts received, approved supplier bills through the cutoff. In the purchasing and receiving routine, she matches each vendor bill to its purchase order and Cora Whitlock’s signed packing slip before entering the bill in QuickBooks. Cora is the Receiving and Inventory Clerk. Elena’s matching of individual packets using the receiving record is not the same thing as my review of the payables report.

**Interviewer:** If the bill, PO and packing slip don’t agree, does Elena decide on the exception herself?

**SME:** The procedure takes a receipt or bill mismatch back to Jalen Sorrell, the Buyer, and Devon Kersey, the Materials and Purchasing Manager. Devon approves exceptions. I wouldn’t present a mismatch as approved merely because it had been sent back. And I wouldn’t invent what a particular resolution was if the supporting records didn’t show one.

**Interviewer:** Is that matching a small part of the month-end summary work you mentioned?

**SME:** It’s separate. There are roughly 480 bill and receipt packets a year, including partial deliveries and invoices. That doesn’t necessarily mean 480 different purchase orders. My rough four hours a month covers the close comparison and reconciliation, selected report exports and re-keying the management summary. It doesn’t include the individual packet matching, and it isn’t the duration of the whole close.

**Interviewer:** Suppose you see the payables report. Can you tell from that report review alone that every packet was matched correctly?

**SME:** I wouldn’t make that claim. The checklist says a report review happened; the individual packet matching belongs to the bill-entry routine. If I needed to establish what happened with a particular bill, I’d need the relevant support rather than taking a checkmark as a reproduction of the underlying packet. The selected checklist doesn’t contain every accounting control or an account-by-account reconciliation.

**Interviewer:** What about bills or adjustments that are not ready at cutoff? Who has authority to clear them?

**SME:** On a bill mismatch, Devon’s exception approval is the authority identified in the procedure. For an adjustment that still needs approval or support, I leave it open and follow up; I don’t post it as approved. I’m not sure who approves every possible kind of adjustment—the records don’t specify a universal approver. It’s important not to turn my responsibility to follow up into an invented blanket approval authority.

## [12:00]

**Interviewer:** Once the cutoff records are together, take me through your review. What are you looking at, and what would make you go back to a source record?

**SME:** In QuickBooks I review the bank report and reconciliation, then the accounts receivable and accounts payable reports. I compare the shipping records with the dealer invoice records for the period. If something doesn’t line up, I go back to the underlying shipping or invoice record and ask Mateo or Elena for the missing detail as appropriate. I don’t have to make up a particular discrepancy to explain that route. The point is that an unresolved item stays open for review.

**Interviewer:** And how does the management summary come out of that?

**SME:** I export selected reports to Excel, then re-key the management summary from the reports. The accounting reports and the summary aren’t the same record. The re-keying sits alongside the comparison and reconciliation work in the roughly four-hour monthly estimate I mentioned. I also review and record approved adjustments as applicable. If an adjustment needs approval or support, I leave it open for follow-up instead of posting it as approved.

**Interviewer:** What does “open” mean for the checklist? Would you initial the step anyway and rely on the summary to explain it later?

**SME:** The checklist is initialed as steps are completed. An unresolved item is left open for review rather than marked complete. [Pause.] I’m not sure of an unrecorded notation or a special form for every unresolved item. What is established is the status: we don’t treat missing detail, support or approval as though it had been supplied.

**Interviewer:** Is there a concrete completed month we can use to see the sequence without assuming it happens identically every time?

**SME:** September 2026 is the completed example. Elena posted shipment invoices through the September 30 cutoff, and posted received approved bills on October 1. I reviewed the bank reconciliation October 2, the receivables and payables reports October 5, and shipping against invoices and the management summary October 6. On October 7 I reviewed adjustments; the note says no approved adjustments were pending. I signed the September checklist that day.

**Interviewer:** Does that last note mean no adjustment was ever proposed or posted in September?

**SME:** No. It says no approved adjustments were pending at that review. I’m not sure what other adjustment activity, if any, occurred; the checklist doesn’t give us a full adjustment ledger. Likewise, the review and signoff are documented, but the checklist doesn’t provide the bank balance, receivable balance or payable balance. I wouldn’t fill them in from a checkoff.

## [16:00]

**Interviewer:** We’re speaking at the October 31 snapshot. How far along is the October close, and what should we avoid inferring from the September example?

**SME:** October’s close was still open at the snapshot. October 31 was a Saturday, and subsequent bank and close ledger work belongs on business days after month-end. We have no later completion or approval asserted here. September’s dates show what was completed for September; they don’t show that the October steps were done, or tell us the date I’ll sign October.

**Interviewer:** So if I asked for October’s closing bank balance, could you give it to me from these records?

**SME:** I’m not sure. The provided checklist is for September, and it doesn’t reproduce account balances. The October close remains open at the snapshot. A missing balance is not a zero balance, and the October shipment count is not a substitute for the bank record.

**Interviewer:** Where does the work tend to require the most careful handoff? I don’t want you to diagnose the company—just describe what you have to have in hand.

**SME:** On the sales side, shipment confirmation needs to reach Elena for the shipment invoice, and then I compare shipping and invoice records. If a detail is missing, I may have to go back to Mateo or Elena rather than finish the comparison on assumption. On the supplier side, Elena needs the PO, Cora’s signed packing slip and the vendor bill; a mismatch goes to Jalen and Devon. For my summary, I’m using selected reports exported to Excel and re-keying from those reports. Each part has its own record and a person responsible for it.

**Interviewer:** And if a manager asks why the checklist isn’t signed yet?

**SME:** I would distinguish an open item from a completed step. For instance, if an adjustment still needs support or approval, it stays open for follow-up. I can’t responsibly describe an actual October adjustment awaiting approval from these files; that’s the rule for handling one, not a documented event. The same care applies to my absence: the records say my review and signoff remain pending, not that someone else automatically signs.

**Interviewer:** What don’t you measure in the material we have?

**SME:** I’m not sure of a complete close-duration history, how often particular mismatches occur, or the time spent resolving them. We have a target, one completed September checklist and an approximate scope-limited four-hour figure. We don’t have all months’ checklists, the full reconciliation or the individual packet matches in that sample. I’d also separate the annual packet estimate from a count of unique POs. Those gaps don’t tell us the measures are zero; they tell us not to claim them.

**Interviewer:** If you had to leave me with one practical distinction, what would it be?

**SME:** A checkmark tells you that the recorded step was completed; it doesn’t contain every piece of evidence behind it. Start with the period and the underlying record, distinguish shipment from an accepted order, and leave an unresolved question open rather than making the checklist look finished. That is how I would explain the work from the information we have.

**Interviewer:** That’s helpful. Thank you, Lydia.

**SME:** You’re welcome.
