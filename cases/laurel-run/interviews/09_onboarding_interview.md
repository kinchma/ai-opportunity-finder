# Onboarding interview — Laurel Run Trailer Works

**Fictional simulation:** This is a constructed interview for an academic case, not an account of an actual conversation.  
**Interviewee:** Nia Hadley, Office and Payroll Administrator  
**Company snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Onboarding, from approved headcount through initial paperwork, access, safety training, and the payroll handoff  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes, estimated from the dialogue and pauses

[00:00]

**Interviewer:** Nia, could you start with where onboarding comes into your work and what would bring a new person to your desk?

**SME:** I keep the onboarding paperwork, and I prepare payroll as well. I report to Lydia Fenner, our Controller. The hiring manager gets headcount approval from Mara Bellamy, our President and CEO, and brings me in on the approved hire, the role, and the person's supervisor. I need those pieces to understand who is doing what. A request for headcount isn't the same thing as approval.

**Interviewer:** So if a manager says they would like another person, is that the point where you start treating the hire as approved?

**SME:** No. I'd want the approval established first. I don't make the headcount decision, and I wouldn't turn an open request into a completed approval on my paperwork. Once the approved hire comes to me, my part includes the forms, the paper onboarding checklist, and the handbook. The employee and supervisor each have work to do too; it isn't all a single office action.

**Interviewer:** What does the employee actually provide, and how do you know when your part is ready to move along?

**SME:** The employee supplies identity and work-eligibility documents through the employer's normal procedure, as well as payroll and contact details. I give them the required forms and follow up on missing items through that procedure. Giving somebody a packet does not tell me the answers are back or that the paperwork is complete. I keep track of what's outstanding on the paper checklist and keep the paperwork secure.

**Interviewer:** Are you checking against a universal list of documents or promising the procedure meets every legal requirement?

**SME:** No. I'm not sure what particular documentation would be required for every possible hire. We follow the applicable requirements for that hire and the normal procedure; the case description isn't a guarantee of legal compliance. And we don't put identity numbers into the sample data. The people who need information for their work should be able to get it without sensitive documents being placed in general sample files.

**Interviewer:** Suppose payroll information is still missing but the handbook has been handed out. How would you describe that status?

**SME:** I'd describe the missing payroll information as outstanding, not say onboarding is complete because a handbook was handed over. Those are different items. I'd follow up through the normal procedure rather than guessing at details or entering a placeholder as though it were the employee's information. [Pause.] It's a small distinction when you say it, but it matters when the checklist is in front of you.

[04:00]

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the practical handoff from those forms to the other records you work with. Where does paper stop and another tool begin?

**SME:** Onboarding uses paper forms and a paper checklist. The office roster and payroll preparation use Excel, and payroll is submitted through RUN Powered by ADP. I use the payroll details for a separate payroll setup. The forms don't turn themselves into payroll setup just because they've been given to the person. I also retain the paperwork securely rather than using a general sample file as a place to hold sensitive material.

**Interviewer:** When you say separate, do you mean the new employee fills something in once and it automatically appears everywhere else?

**SME:** No automatic transfer is established in the records. The onboarding forms and the payroll setup are separate parts of the work. I need the details for payroll, but I wouldn't describe the paper checklist as the payroll system. Nor would I assume every item is finished just because one of the records has been started.

**Interviewer:** What if a person asks you exactly where a particular document is stored, how long it stays there, or who can retrieve it?

**SME:** I'm not sure of a specific storage location, retention period, or complete access list from these records. What I can say is that the paperwork is kept securely and information is available to the people who need it for their work. I wouldn't fill in a cabinet, a folder permission, or a retention rule just to make the answer sound more complete.

**Interviewer:** Who else is involved before somebody can use the company's systems? Imagine the employee needs a Windows PC and some shared-drive material for their job.

**SME:** An access request is on paper and needs approval. The approved request goes to Simon Denby, our Facilities and IT Coordinator. Simon provisions only the access approved for the job, and he maintains the Excel device and access list. We use Windows PCs and a shared office drive, but being hired doesn't automatically give a person access to every system or file.

**Interviewer:** Who has authority to approve a particular access request?

**SME:** I'm not sure; the process says the request must be approved, but it doesn't name a universal approver for each kind of access. I can tell you Simon handles provisioning of approved access. I can't say that he, the hiring manager, or I can approve every request without more information.

**Interviewer:** And if the role needs something but that request hasn't been approved yet?

**SME:** Leave that access pending and get the approval. We don't bypass the request because the employee has started or because somebody believes the access will probably be needed. If Simon isn't available to help, it stays pending rather than somebody else granting unapproved access. I would distinguish the need for access from the authority to give it.

[08:00]

**Interviewer:** Let's turn to the first work at the site. What is the supervisor's responsibility, as opposed to yours?

**SME:** The supervisor introduces the employee to the site, conducts the safety training needed for the assigned tasks, and signs off. I record completion on the onboarding checklist and retain the forms securely. So I rely on the supervisor's part being done and signed off; I don't replace it by giving out the handbook. The employee's work matters here, because the training needed is for the tasks assigned to that person.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me a concrete example of how you'd identify the supervisor, without suggesting there is a current new hire in the records?

**SME:** Yes. Theo Lowry is the Fabrication Supervisor and supervises Micah Orlin, a Fabricator/Welder. Avery Carver is the Assembly Supervisor and supervises the assembly and finish staff listed under Avery. Those are examples of reporting relationships in the roster. They don't tell us that Micah or anybody on Avery's list is a new hire or that a particular onboarding was completed.

**Interviewer:** What if the person has the handbook and some of the forms are returned, but the training signoff is still outstanding?

**SME:** Leave that item open and follow up with the person responsible. Work requiring the task-specific training should wait until the training is done and signed off. Having a handbook isn't the same as completing that training. [Pause.] I can have part of the paper packet accounted for without having the safety portion finished; I don't need to force those into one status.

**Interviewer:** Would you be the person deciding precisely which hazards and skills are covered for every job?

**SME:** I'm not sure of a complete training curriculum from the records, and I'm not the person described as conducting the supervisor's training. I'd look to the supervisor for what the assigned tasks require and for the signoff. I can explain the handoff and record completion; I can't certify details that haven't been supplied to me.

**Interviewer:** Simon also handles building issues. If a supervisor encounters an urgent hazard while all this other coordination is happening, how does that relate to waiting for a routine request?

**SME:** The supervisor reports an urgent hazard immediately to Owen Bellamy, the Plant Manager, and takes unsafe equipment out of service. Routine access or maintenance scheduling must not delay that response. That's separate from leaving an ordinary access request pending; a safety hazard isn't just another item waiting for Simon's calendar.

[12:00]

**Interviewer:** Once you have what you need for the payroll side, what would you explain to an employee about recording time?

**SME:** I would make sure the person knows which time record applies. Hourly staff use paper time cards for Sunday through Saturday workweeks. Salaried staff submit paper exception notices, not hourly cards. At the snapshot there are 50 employees: 36 hourly and 14 salaried. The record used matters; I shouldn't take an estimated salaried-hours figure and call it a completed time card.

**Interviewer:** Is the employee's supervisor always the person approving an hourly card?

**SME:** Supervisors approve their direct reports' cards, and the relevant managers approve the other four hourly staff. I don't assume I am the approver just because I collect the cards. The reporting relationship tells you where the supervisor handoff goes for the direct reports; for an example, Theo supervises Micah. The records don't name an alternate time-card approver if the usual person is unavailable, so I'm not sure who would fill that role.

**Interviewer:** And what happens after you collect the cards?

**SME:** Every 14 days I collect them, resolve omissions, and enter the approved weekly regular and overtime hours. I check the entries and totals against the cards, review totals with Lydia, and then submit payroll through RUN Powered by ADP. It's a biweekly process, with 26 periods in a year. I keep the two Sunday–Saturday weeks separate rather than using one two-week total in place of the weekly amounts.

**Interviewer:** Why does that distinction make a difference? Could you use the supplied example?

**SME:** The illustrative period is 2026-10-18 through 2026-10-31. Micah's example shows 40 hours in the first week and 42 in the second, including two overtime hours in that second week. If I saw only the combined number, I wouldn't have the same weekly breakdown in front of me. It's an example of the later payroll handoff, not a new-hire record and not an annual payroll ledger.

**Interviewer:** What had actually been approved or paid in that example as of the snapshot?

**SME:** Theo's approval of Micah's card on 2026-11-02 and the 2026-11-05 payday were planned, not completed as of 2026-10-31. I wouldn't tell a new employee that an approval or payday happened just because the example names a future date. Nor would I use this example as a general legal interpretation of payroll.

**Interviewer:** If a card is missing or has an unclear time or signature, what can you do with it?

**SME:** I check with the employee and approver. If a card needs correction, it goes back for signoff; unresolved cases go to Lydia. A missing card isn't zero hours. It can take a bit of back-and-forth to know what to enter, especially when a figure or signature isn't clear. That's not something I can settle by guessing.

[16:00]

**Interviewer:** I'd like to bring the pieces back together. If an approved hire has supplied the payroll details, the supervisor has not signed training, and access is still awaiting approval, how would you describe that situation?

**SME:** I'd describe each part separately. I can use the supplied payroll details for the separate setup, subject to whatever else is needed there. The training remains open until the supervisor completes and signs it off, and work requiring that training waits. The access stays pending until it's approved; Simon provisions what has actually been approved. I wouldn't call the whole process complete merely because one part is ready.

**Interviewer:** Is there a standard number of days in the records for completing onboarding, or a report showing how long those handoffs usually take?

**SME:** I'm not sure. The materials describe the procedure, but they don't give me an onboarding completion-time measure, a standard deadline for all hires, or a report of how many items are overdue. I can tell you what remains open in the situation you've described, not a measured average or a promised completion date. A blank or absent measure wouldn't mean the time was zero.

**Interviewer:** What about a count of incomplete files at the snapshot or a typical reason for a delay?

**SME:** I'm not sure. We have a working procedure and a paper checklist, but the supplied records don't establish that count or a distribution of reasons. Missing forms, a pending access request, and an unsigned training item are useful illustrations of how we handle different open items; they aren't evidence that any of those happened to a particular employee or that one is the most common.

**Interviewer:** If you are away when a hire needs attention, who takes over your onboarding work?

**SME:** The records don't name a standing onboarding backup. I'd check with Lydia about coverage and keep the forms secure; I'm not sure who would be assigned. The records also don't name a backup payroll submitter. I wouldn't present a colleague as the designated replacement just because they work in the office.

**Interviewer:** Looking at your own part of the work, what requires the most care when you're explaining the status to someone else?

**SME:** Being precise about what I know. A request isn't approval, a packet handed out isn't paperwork returned, and my recording a supervisor's signoff isn't the same thing as conducting training. Then there is the later payroll distinction: an approved weekly amount isn't simply any number on an incomplete card. The paper and the separate records each tell part of the story, so I need to know which part I'm talking about.

**Interviewer:** And if the documents don't establish something?

**SME:** I'd say I'm not sure and check with the person responsible rather than fill the gap with an assumption. That includes dates, who may approve a particular access request, and who covers a role when someone is away.

**Interviewer:** That's helpful. Thank you for walking through the boundaries and the handoffs.

**SME:** You're welcome.

*End of simulated interview, approximately 20:00.*
