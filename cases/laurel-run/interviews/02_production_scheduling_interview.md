# Production scheduling — simulated interview

**Fictional disclosure:** This is an invented interview based on the Laurel Run Trailer Works case, not a transcript of an actual conversation.  
**Interviewee:** Jonah Sutter, Production Scheduler  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Production scheduling and shop-floor handoffs  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes

## [00:00]

**Interviewer:** Could you start with your part in getting a trailer onto the shop schedule? Where does your responsibility begin, and who owns the floor once the work is there?

**SME:** I’m Jonah Sutter. I report to Owen Bellamy, our plant manager. I keep the production schedule in Excel and the shop whiteboard. Owen owns the floor. My job is to take confirmed work, look at what we can put in sequence, get the paper packets to the floor, and keep the schedule and board current as the supervisors report progress or exceptions.

**Interviewer:** When you say confirmed work, what has to happen before you consider an order schedulable?

**SME:** A quote by itself is still an offer. Priya Ellwood receives the dealer’s written acceptance of an approved quote and enters the order in the Excel order register. She copies the approved model and options into the register and the paper build packet. That’s the point of departure for me: an approved quote that the dealer has accepted, not just a conversation about what a dealer might want.

**Interviewer:** And what do you review when you set up the week?

**SME:** The approved orders, the stock notes, and input from Theo Lowry in fabrication and Avery Carver in assembly. I assign a separate work order and a target date to each trailer. Then I sequence the confirmed work orders in Excel and on the whiteboard and give the packets to the floor. The packet goes through fabrication, paint, assembly and inspection. Theo and Avery bring progress and material exceptions back to me.

**Interviewer:** If one dealer order covers more than one trailer, does the order number do all the identifying?

**SME:** No. Each trailer still gets its own work order. The order number and work-order number are different sequences, and neither one is a VIN. That distinction matters when we’re talking about the dealer’s order on one side and a particular piece of work in the shop on the other.

**Interviewer:** Is there a fixed formula that tells you exactly where each work order belongs?

**SME:** I’m not sure there’s a documented formula in these records. I can tell you the inputs I check, but I wouldn’t give you a made-up priority rule. The stock notes and what Theo and Avery tell me matter to the sequence. The target I put against the work is an estimate, not a guarantee.

## [04:00]

**Interviewer:** Could you walk me through an actual case example, from acceptance to the later outcome, without assuming anything the record doesn’t show?

**SME:** Cedar Bend Trailer Sales is a good one. Its dealer key is `DLR-0001`. The request was for one Trailfield with a spare-tire mount only. Priya prepared `QTE-2026-1180` on October 14, 2026; Tessa Bellamy approved the $4,920 dealer total. The dealer accepted on October 15. The resulting order was `ORD-2026-1120`, and I scheduled `WO-2026-1240`, dated October 15.

**Interviewer:** Why emphasize mount only?

**SME:** Because that’s what was approved in this example. I wouldn’t turn it into a spare tire as well, or assume a different option from the name alone. The model and approved options need to carry from the accepted quote into the order and the paper build packet. This sample shows no revision, so I can’t use it as an example of a changed specification.

**Interviewer:** What can you say about the dates at the other end?

**SME:** The example records finish inspection on October 26 and shipment on October 27, 2026. Those are actual events in the fictional record; the work-order date is not the shipment date. Mateo Renshaw schedules shipment, and Elena Corwin issues the dealer invoice at shipment after shipping confirmation. I’m responsible for the production schedule, not for treating a planned date as proof a dealer has received anything.

**Interviewer:** Was October 27 the original target date you assigned?

**SME:** I’m not sure. The example gives the work-order date, finish-inspection date and shipment date, but it doesn’t give my original target. It would be wrong to fill that blank with the shipment date or to say whether this one was early or late.

**Interviewer:** Once a packet has gone to the floor, what changes your view of where work stands?

**SME:** Reports from Theo and Avery. If they report progress or a material exception, I update the Excel schedule and the board as the order of work changes. The records are separate: Priya has the order register, Devon Kersey has the inventory sheet, I have the schedule, and the paper packet moves through the shop. So when somebody asks what’s happening, the order entry and my schedule aren’t the same thing as a completed inspection or a shipping confirmation.

**Interviewer:** Does that mean you can read current shipment status straight from the order register?

**SME:** Not as a substitute for checking. Priya checks with me or Mateo for current status when a dealer follows up. I can speak to the schedule; Mateo is the shipping coordinator. If a promised date moves, I tell Priya so she can update the dealer. [Pause.] That’s an awkward conversation when a date has moved, but it’s better to distinguish an estimate from something that has actually shipped.

## [08:00]

**Interviewer:** Let’s take a change to a trailer already on the schedule. What starts the change process, and who has authority at each point?

**SME:** If the dealer wants a specification change, Tessa obtains the dealer’s approval. A proposed change isn’t approved just because somebody has discussed it. Once it is approved, I issue a dated replacement packet page. Theo and Avery return the old page, and Iris Calder checks the current revision at inspection.

**Interviewer:** Suppose an older copy is still in somebody’s hand. What should happen before they use it?

**SME:** Check it against the changed page rather than building from the older copy. The current dated page is the one that belongs in the packet. The written dealer approval stays with the email, and the order remains in the register. You want the person working from paper to have the approved revision, not just a memory that a change was mentioned.

**Interviewer:** Do the records tell us how often that comparison comes up?

**SME:** They describe 24 examples in the year where a changed page needs verification against an older copy. That tells us about the comparison, not that 24 incorrect trailers were built. I wouldn’t infer the outcome of each example from that count.

**Interviewer:** Who decides whether a different component can be used if the original one is hard to get? Is that your call as scheduler?

**SME:** No. Before a material substitution, Felix Harrow, our manufacturing engineer, and Iris need to confirm fit and required specifications. Brakes, tires, couplers, lighting and substitutions need fit and applicable compliance review. I can adjust the sequence while a material question is being checked; I can’t simply authorize a substitute to protect a date.

**Interviewer:** If a component has a rating stamped on it, does that settle what load the trailer can carry?

**SME:** No. A component rating alone doesn’t establish trailer payload. Use the completed unit’s certification and load labels. That’s another place where a scheduling shorthand could give the wrong answer if we let it stand in for the completed trailer’s information.

**Interviewer:** What detail about revision control can’t you establish from the records?

**SME:** I’m not sure, for example, whether there’s a separate signoff for every replacement page; the records describe the dated page, the return of the old page and Iris’s check. I can explain those steps without claiming there’s another approval or a separate system that isn’t shown.

## [12:00]

**Interviewer:** Now a shortage: how does it reach you, and what do you do with it?

**SME:** Theo or Avery tells me material is short. I check the stock notes and expected receipts with Devon, our materials and purchasing manager, and adjust the sequence. Useful alternative work may be assigned. The point is to know what we can put through the shop without pretending the missing material is there or using an unconfirmed substitute.

**Interviewer:** Can you give a documented event, and tell me what it does and doesn’t show?

**SME:** On September 16, 2026, the shortage record lists `SKU-0012`, a floor-screw box. Three workers were held for three hours each before reassignment to other work. That’s nine staff-hours in that event. The record says no substitution was made and no safety compromise occurred. It does not say the whole plant stopped.

**Interviewer:** Do you know the specific order affected, why the box was short, or what the workers were reassigned to?

**SME:** I’m not sure. Those details aren’t in that event record. The next action is described as reassignment to other work, but the record doesn’t name that work. It also doesn’t establish that a particular dealer shipment was late. I wouldn’t attach the Cedar Bend order to the shortage just because both are in the case files.

**Interviewer:** Was that the only shortage shown for the year?

**SME:** No. The file has four separate events during the reporting year. In each, three workers were held for three hours each before reassignment. No substitution or safety compromise is recorded in those events. They’re examples of shortages, not a complete history of inventory movements or a measure of every minute the shop might have been waiting.

**Interviewer:** When you check with Devon, what are you trying to settle for the schedule?

**SME:** Whether the stock notes and expected receipts support the work I had in sequence, and what else might usefully move. Devon reviews counts and expected receipts; the inventory sheet is separate from my Excel schedule. If the production order changes, I have to update the schedule and the board from the information I’m given. If a promised date moves, Priya needs to know so she can update the dealer.

**Interviewer:** Is a supplier’s expected receipt the same thing as material received and ready to use?

**SME:** No. An expected receipt is an expectation. I wouldn’t tell someone it has arrived simply because it appears in the notes as expected. I’m not sure of the receipt timing for that September shortage, so I can’t reconstruct when it was resolved from this record.

**Interviewer:** And if the issue is an immediate safety hazard rather than a shortage?

**SME:** The supervisor tells Owen immediately, and unsafe equipment is taken out of service. That’s the stated response; the shortage examples don’t record a safety compromise. Keeping an estimate on the board doesn’t override that response.

## [16:00]

**Interviewer:** What can the production log tell us about the workload you’re scheduling, and what can’t it tell us?

**SME:** For November 1, 2025 through October 31, 2026, the unit log has 1,500 completions and 1,500 shipments. It shows 125 completions and 125 shipments in each calendar month. In October, completions were 50 Trailfield, 45 Enclosure and 30 Workspan; the Cedar Bend trailer is included. Those are recorded monthly counts, not an order-by-order schedule or a guarantee for the next week.

**Interviewer:** The process description also says about 30 trailers per working week over 50 working weeks. How should I use that alongside the monthly log?

**SME:** As a description of the reporting-year scale, not a promise that every week was exactly the same. The month is the unit of the log, and the weekly figure is approximate. The even monthly counts don’t establish seasonality. Nor do completion and shipment totals being equal tell you that every unit completed in a particular month shipped in that month.

**Interviewer:** Do those records show what share of trailers met their original target dates?

**SME:** I’m not sure. I don’t see the original targets and actual outcomes linked across the full set of orders in the unit log. A total completed is useful for counting output, but it doesn’t answer an on-time question by itself. The Cedar Bend sample has a work-order date and actual later dates, but, as we said, no original target is shown there either.

**Interviewer:** What happens when a dealer wants an update before a trailer ships?

**SME:** Priya handles the dealer conversation. She uses the order register and checks with me or Mateo for current status. The process records describe around 300 dealer order-status follow-ups in a year, separate from warranty and stockout inquiries. That’s a count of follow-ups, not a count of late trailers or unique orders. When a promised date moves, I tell her so she can relay the change.

**Interviewer:** What does that feel like in the ordinary course of your work?

**SME:** The practical difficulty is that a target can move while the dealer still needs an answer. I need the supervisors’ progress and material information to keep my schedule and the board current, and Priya needs the current picture rather than an old estimate. The records don’t tell me how long each follow-up takes or whether every inquiry produces a date change, so I wouldn’t put numbers on those things.

**Interviewer:** If you were unavailable, who is the designated backup scheduler? And what will actual output be next November?

**SME:** I’m not sure on either. The company records don’t name a separate backup scheduler, and they don’t establish actual future output. That doesn’t prove nobody could help with the work; it just means I can’t identify a designated backup from what we have.

**Interviewer:** Before we close, where should someone look for the current working evidence, rather than treating this conversation as the record?

**SME:** Keep the written dealer approval with the email, the order in the register, the schedule in Excel and the current dated page in the paper packet. Iris keeps the final-inspection checklist with that packet. For a shipment, look for shipping confirmation rather than relying on my target date. Those records do different jobs, and the difference matters when somebody asks where a trailer actually stands.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Jonah. That’s a helpful place to stop.
