# Simulated interview: Facilities upkeep

**Fictional disclosure:** This is a constructed, text-only interview for the fictional Laurel Run Trailer Works, Inc. It is not a transcript of an actual interview.  
**Interviewee:** Simon Denby, Facilities and IT Coordinator  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Building, equipment service, and ordinary office upkeep at the Hollow Glen factory  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes, including natural pauses; timestamps are approximate.

## [00:00]

**Interviewer:** Simon, could you start by describing what facilities upkeep means in your job, and where your responsibility starts and stops?

**SME:** I coordinate service for the building and equipment and keep the maintenance list. I also have IT work: devices, an Excel device and access list, and coordination with an outside IT support contractor. I report to Owen Bellamy, our Plant Manager. He owns the floor, so an urgent hazard on the floor isn't something to park with me as a routine service request.

**Interviewer:** What sort of operation are you supporting?

**SME:** We have one factory in Hollow Glen. Cutting, welding, paint, final assembly, inspection, inventory and administration are there. I'm looking at building and equipment service across that setting, not claiming I run every department inside it. Holly Darnell handles reception and office supplies. Supervisors have their own responsibilities on the floor.

**Interviewer:** How do the facilities and IT parts fit together in practice?

**SME:** They're both part of the same job. The approximate time allocation is 45 percent IT, 45 percent facilities and administration, and 10 percent production and operations. Those are working-time estimates, not separate jobs or a schedule that says I'll be available for each function at a particular hour. Sometimes a small maintenance request waits until I get back from IT work.

**Interviewer:** Say someone has an ordinary maintenance request. Walk me from the first notification to whatever you do with it.

**SME:** The routine route is Outlook. Give me enough detail to identify the place or equipment and the work needed. I can then follow it through the Excel maintenance list and coordinate a service provider as needed. I update the list as appropriate. The list helps me see scheduled service and what still needs attention.

**Interviewer:** Is every email a service appointment as soon as it arrives?

**SME:** No. A request tells me there's something to look at or coordinate; it isn't itself evidence that a provider has been scheduled or the work finished. [Pause.] And this is the ordinary route. If it's an urgent hazard, it needs the immediate supervisor route, not an email waiting for me.

**Interviewer:** What happens if the request doesn't identify where the problem is?

**SME:** I'd need enough information to identify the location or equipment and the work needed before I could sensibly coordinate it. I'm not sure the records prescribe a particular request form or a required set of fields beyond that detail. The point is to make the request understandable, not to pretend an incomplete note tells us everything.

## [04:00]

**Interviewer:** Could you give an example involving Holly, and make clear what is an example rather than a repair you've documented?

**SME:** Sure. If Holly notices an ordinary, nonurgent maintenance item during an office round, she can send me the details in Outlook. I follow it up through the maintenance list and arrange service if needed. That's an illustration of the route, not a dated incident or a claim that a particular repair happened.

**Interviewer:** What if what she notices is simply that office supplies need ordering?

**SME:** Office supplies are Holly's job to order. They don't become an equipment-service request just because she sees them on a round. She also receives visitors and keeps reception supplies on hand. My maintenance coordination and her office administration touch the same workplace, but they're not the same task.

**Interviewer:** How do her rounds relate to the contract cleaners?

**SME:** Holly collects office wastebaskets on her daily reception rounds. Ordinary rounds take about two hours a week across an approximately 50-week working year. Contract cleaners also serve the company, but they aren't Laurel Run employees. I'm not sure of their particular schedule, and the records don't assign Holly's wastebasket collection to them. I wouldn't assume either that they do it or that they never do anything else.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned the maintenance list. What is it useful for, and what shouldn't someone expect it to answer?

**SME:** It tracks scheduled building and equipment service and helps identify what still needs attention. It's an Excel list, not a blanket authorization to use equipment, and it doesn't replace our paper safety and building inspection records. I'm not sure it establishes the full history of every request, every inspection or every repair. I wouldn't use it to claim those things without the supporting records.

**Interviewer:** What do you keep on paper?

**SME:** Safety and building inspection records. Keep those papers with the inspection records; the Excel list is for tracking service, not a substitute for the papers. The records I have to work from don't specify an inspection interval or a particular filing location. I'm not sure what either would be, so I wouldn't make one up.

**Interviewer:** Suppose I'm trying to tell whether a service is scheduled or merely needs attention. Can I just assume one from the other?

**SME:** No. Those are different states. The list is there to help see both; a request or a need for attention isn't proof an appointment has been made. Equally, scheduling service isn't proof that the work has been completed. What the list actually tells you depends on what has been entered and updated.

## [08:00]

**Interviewer:** Let's shift to something more serious. What should happen when someone spots an urgent hazard?

**SME:** Tell the supervisor immediately. The supervisor reports it immediately to Owen, and unsafe equipment is taken out of service. Don't leave it in the routine Outlook queue. I can coordinate service as needed, but arranging service isn't the immediate safety response.

**Interviewer:** Could you make that concrete using the floor roles?

**SME:** If there's an urgent hazard, Theo Lowry, the Fabrication Supervisor, or Avery Carver, the Assembly Supervisor, would report it to Owen rather than wait for me to finish an IT job. That describes the route; it isn't a report that either person encountered a particular hazard on a particular day.

**Interviewer:** If you're away and an urgent item comes up, does someone wait until you're back to decide what to do?

**SME:** No. The immediate report still goes to the supervisor and then Owen. Unsafe equipment is taken out of service. My availability to coordinate a provider is separate from that immediate response. The records don't name a separate facilities backup, so I'm not going to name a person to fill one in.

**Interviewer:** And what about a routine, nonurgent item while you're away?

**SME:** Use Outlook for my return. That distinction matters: an ordinary request can follow the routine route, but an urgent hazard shouldn't wait in that queue. If a person is reporting one, the prompt supervisor-to-Owen handoff is what the procedure calls for.

**Interviewer:** What happens before unsafe equipment goes back into use?

**SME:** Keep it out of service while the hazard is addressed and follow applicable safety procedures before returning it to use. The maintenance list is a scheduling record. An entry on it isn't permission to use unsafe equipment.

**Interviewer:** Who has the final release authority? Does a vendor finishing work automatically give that permission?

**SME:** I'm not sure who has a specific release approval; the available records don't specify one. Nor do they say that a provider finishing work automatically authorizes return to use. We shouldn't infer an approval from an appointment or from my coordination role. Follow the applicable safety procedures rather than treating the list as a release.

**Interviewer:** Is there a documented emergency response time or a detailed set of steps for every type of hazard?

**SME:** I'm not sure. The procedure tells us to report immediately, take unsafe equipment out of service and follow applicable safety procedures. It doesn't give a numerical response-time target or a separate detailed treatment for every possible hazard. I can describe the route we have; I can't invent the rest.

## [12:00]

**Interviewer:** How do scheduled services enter your work, as distinct from a request that just arrived?

**SME:** I track scheduled building and equipment service in Excel and coordinate with service providers. The list is where I can see scheduled items alongside things that still need attention. A routine Outlook request is another way a need comes to me. Depending on what's needed, I can coordinate a provider and update the list as appropriate.

**Interviewer:** Do the records give you a prescribed interval for each machine or building inspection?

**SME:** I'm not sure. They say I track scheduled service and retain the inspection records; they don't specify particular service or inspection intervals. I can't turn the existence of a schedule into a claim that every item is serviced on a particular cycle.

**Interviewer:** How much can you tell me about the service providers themselves?

**SME:** I coordinate facilities service providers and an outside IT support contractor. They're outside contractors, not employees. I retain vendor contact details, but I'm not sure of the IT contractor's name from the available records, and no particular contractor price is specified for this process. I wouldn't supply a name, price, or response promise as though it were recorded.

**Interviewer:** Is there a documented approval or spending threshold for arranging a facilities service?

**SME:** I'm not sure. My role is coordination, and Owen is my manager, but the available procedure doesn't specify a facilities spending threshold or who approves each service expense. Reporting to Owen doesn't by itself establish that every appointment requires his approval, or that I can approve every bill. Those are different questions.

**Interviewer:** Suppose someone asks you for a completed service record. Would a scheduled entry be sufficient?

**SME:** I wouldn't present a scheduled entry as proof of completed work. I'd distinguish what's scheduled, what needs attention and what the available record actually establishes. If the question is about a safety or building inspection, the paper inspection records matter too; the Excel maintenance list doesn't replace them.

**Interviewer:** Can you describe the exact fields on that list, how it is organized, or where every service document is stored?

**SME:** I'm not sure. I know its purpose and that it's in Excel, but the provided records don't give a column-by-column layout or a full service-document filing procedure. They do say to keep safety and building inspection papers with the inspection records. I wouldn't infer a work-order number, completion signature or digital inspection archive from that.

**Interviewer:** So a person comparing an email, the list and a paper inspection record should be careful about what each one is evidence of?

**SME:** Exactly. The Outlook message is the routine request route, the Excel list tracks service and attention, and inspection records are retained on paper. [Pause.] They're related to the work, but they aren't interchangeable evidence, and a missing detail in one isn't proof that the event didn't happen.

## [16:00]

**Interviewer:** I'd like to understand the switching you mentioned. What is actually known about that workload?

**SME:** As a rough guide, around 24 routine maintenance and IT handoffs in a year involve about 20 minutes of switching and coordination apiece. That's a guide to those handoffs, not a count of all maintenance requests, and the 20 minutes isn't total repair time. It's also separate from Holly's rounds and from emergency response.

**Interviewer:** When someone hears “20 minutes,” they might think the repair itself took that long. Is that what you mean?

**SME:** No. It's switching and coordination associated with the handoff. It doesn't tell us how long a provider spent repairing anything, how long the equipment was unavailable, or how long a requester waited. Some small requests wait until I return from IT work, but I don't have a measured waiting-time distribution to give you.

**Interviewer:** Does that handoff figure tell you how many requests are open right now or whether the schedule is being met?

**SME:** No. I'm not sure of an open-request count, completion rate or backlog age from the records available here. The maintenance list helps identify scheduled service and things needing attention, but that rough annual handoff guide isn't a service-level report. A missing measure isn't a zero.

**Interviewer:** What about Holly's two hours? Is that included in your handoff figure?

**SME:** No, they're separate. Her approximate two hours a week describe ordinary reception rounds, including collecting office wastebaskets, across an approximately 50-week working year. The handoff guide concerns routine maintenance and IT switching and coordination. Neither number tells you the contract cleaners' hours or the duration of an emergency response.

**Interviewer:** If you are managing a device issue and a routine request arrives, what should the requester understand about the handoff?

**SME:** A small request may wait until I'm back from IT work. The requester should still provide the location or equipment and the work needed in Outlook so I can follow it up. I'm not sure of a promised response time or a named facilities backup. For an urgent hazard, though, don't apply that waiting arrangement: tell the supervisor immediately.

**Interviewer:** If I asked you how many repairs failed, how much downtime they caused, or whether all planned maintenance was completed on time, could you give me reliable figures here?

**SME:** I'm not sure. The supplied process gives responsibilities, routes and the rough handoff guide, but not those measures or a complete repair history. I wouldn't infer that there were no failed repairs or no downtime. I'd want the relevant records before making a claim about either.

**Interviewer:** Before we close, what's the most important distinction for someone new to the process?

**SME:** Ordinary maintenance goes to me through Outlook with enough detail, and I coordinate and track service as appropriate. Urgent hazards go immediately through the supervisor to Owen, with unsafe equipment taken out of service and applicable safety procedures followed before return. Holly's supply and reception work, my IT work, and the inspection papers each have their own place in the picture. Don't confuse the maintenance list with a safety release.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Simon. That gives us a clear picture of what the records do and don't establish.

*Closing: approximately 20 minutes.*
