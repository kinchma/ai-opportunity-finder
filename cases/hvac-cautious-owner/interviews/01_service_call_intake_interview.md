# Service Call Intake - Interview with Marisol Quintero

Interviewee: Marisol Quintero, Customer Service Rep and Backup Dispatcher (since 2019)
Date: 2026-10-27
Fictional transcript. About 18 minutes. Timestamps are approximate.

## [00:00] Role and a normal day

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Marisol. Can you start with what your job is and what a normal day looks like?

**SME:** Sure. I'm one of the two customer service reps, me and Jolene. I answer the phones, book service calls, and I'm the backup dispatcher. So when Trevor goes to lunch or he's out, I run the board. I've been here since 2019. Most of my day is the phone. We open at 7:30 and from about 7:45 it's pretty steady until five.

**Interviewer:** Roughly how many calls do you take in a day?

**SME:** On a normal day I'd say 50 to 70 calls between the two of us, and in a heat wave or a cold snap it's a lot more. Not all of them are new service calls. A lot of them are people asking when the tech is coming, or a tech calling in, or somebody wanting to pay a bill over the phone.

**Interviewer:** Of those, how many are new service requests?

**SME:** We book about 20 to 30 service calls on a regular day. Over the year it's about 5,200 service calls, that's the number Dana gives at the meeting. Plus the tune-ups in spring and fall, but those are a different thing, Jolene mostly runs those.

**Interviewer:** And when someone calls to ask where the tech is, how do you find out?

**SME:** I walk over to the board. Literally. The dispatch room is right behind us, so I put them on hold, go look at the magnets, maybe ask Trevor if the tech called in, and come back. In summer that's 30, 40 calls a day just asking when we'll be there. It's not hard, it's just a lot of getting up.

## [03:30] Booking a phone call

**Interviewer:** Walk me through what happens when a new customer calls for service.

**SME:** First I look them up in QuickBooks, the customer list. If they've used us before, the address and phone are there. If they're new, I take the name, address, phone and email. Then I ask what kind of equipment they have and what's going on. I ask if they're a Comfort Club member, and I check the member list in the Comfort Club tab of the dispatch spreadsheet, because people don't always remember.

**Interviewer:** And then?

**SME:** I tell them the service call is $99, and that if they go ahead with the repair the visit fee goes toward it, and members get 15% off the repair. Then I add a row to the dispatch spreadsheet. Date, customer, address, phone, equipment, the problem, member yes or no, and priority. No heat or no cooling is P1. Then I tell them Trevor will call with a window, or if Trevor's right there I just give them the window.

**Interviewer:** So the customer is looked up in QuickBooks and then typed into the spreadsheet.

**SME:** Yes. They don't talk to each other. If it's a new customer, Dana adds them in QuickBooks later from the ticket. So technically a new customer gets typed in twice, once by me in the spreadsheet and once by Dana when she does the invoice.

**Interviewer:** Do you ever get duplicates?

**SME:** All the time, honestly. Somebody fills out the web form and then calls because nobody called them back, and now they're in there twice. The procedure says search by phone number before you add a row. I do, mostly. When it's busy you miss some.

## [07:00] After-hours messages

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the after-hours messages.

**SME:** After five, and weekends, the calls go to Midstate Answering. They take a message and email it to the service@ inbox. If it's no heat or no cooling, they also text the on-call tech. So first thing when I come in at 7:30, I open service@, print the messages from overnight, and go through them.

**Interviewer:** What do you do with each one?

**SME:** I check whether the on-call tech already went out. I'll ask Trevor if he got a text, or call the on-call guy. Then for every one that's still open, I type it into the dispatch spreadsheet as a new row and I call the customer back to confirm. The printouts go in the Answering Service folder.

**Interviewer:** How many messages is that on a typical morning?

**SME:** Average about four. It's about 1,050 messages a year, Dana counted. But it's lumpy. A Monday in January after a cold weekend, I've had 25. That Monday takes me most of the morning before I can get to the regular phones, and the phones are ringing the whole time. On a normal day it's maybe 20 minutes, on a bad Monday 45 minutes or more just typing and calling.

**Interviewer:** Is the Midstate email in a consistent format?

**SME:** Kind of. They type what the customer says, so it's name, address, phone, and then whatever the person said. Sometimes the address is wrong or the phone has a digit missing. Sometimes it's somebody who just wants to book a tune-up and called at night. Those wait until morning anyway.

**Interviewer:** Give me an example of one that went out overnight.

**SME:** The one I always remember is January, Patricia Hollins in Westerville, furnace blowing cold air at two in the morning, small kids. Midstate texted Derek, he was on call, he went at about 3:10. In the morning I typed it in so Dana could match his ticket, and I put a follow-up call on it. That one worked the way it should.

## [11:00] Web form requests

**Interviewer:** And the website form?

**SME:** That goes to service@ too. Name, phone, address, message. I check service@ when the phones are quiet. In winter and summer the phones are never quiet, so honestly the web requests sit. I call them back, and if they don't answer I leave a message and reply to the email. Once I get them, it's booked like a phone call.

**Interviewer:** How long does it usually take to get back to a web request?

**SME:** Dana looked at it once for a meeting, and it was about a day and a half on average, business days. In peak season it's longer, two or three days sometimes. A request that comes in Friday afternoon, I might not see until Monday. Like that one in September, the AC grinding noise, came in Friday at 4:40 and I called Monday morning.

**Interviewer:** Do you hear from customers about that?

**SME:** Sometimes. I'll call back and they say "oh, we already had somebody out." Or they call in mad that nobody responded. The procedure says apologize, book them first, and tell Kyle. I do tell Kyle. I'm not sure how many we lose that way, nobody tracks it. It's about 410 web requests a year, so even a few a month adds up.

**Interviewer:** Is there anyone whose job is to watch that inbox?

**SME:** Not really. It's whoever has a minute. Me, Jolene, sometimes Dana sees one and forwards it. It's the same inbox the answering service uses, plus vendors, plus spam.

## [14:30] Backup dispatch and tools

**Interviewer:** You also cover dispatch. How does that fit with intake?

**SME:** It actually works fine, because I already know who's waiting. If I booked a P1 that morning, I know it's there when I take over the board. Trevor and I sit next to each other, so I hear what's going on. When I'm on dispatch, Jolene takes all the phones, which is a lot for one person.

**Interviewer:** Have you used Housecall Pro?

**SME:** Kyle puts the next day's calls in it so customers get a reminder text. I've never used it for booking. We were supposed to switch to it a couple of years ago, I think 2024, but the techs didn't want to do tickets on the phone, so it stopped. I'm not sure what else it does.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about intake, what would it be?

**SME:** Not having to type the same thing three times. The answering service types it, I type it, Dana types it again. And not walking to the board 40 times a day. But I'm not complaining, it's a good place to work.

**Interviewer:** Anything I didn't ask that I should know?

**SME:** Just that winter Mondays are the hardest day of the week for us. If you want to see the system at its worst, that's when.

**Interviewer:** Thanks, Marisol.
