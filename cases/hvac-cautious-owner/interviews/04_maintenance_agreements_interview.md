# Maintenance Agreements - Interview with Wes Delacroix

Interviewee: Wes Delacroix, Commercial Accounts Manager (since 2012)
Date: 2026-10-28
Fictional transcript. About 20 minutes. Timestamps are approximate.

## [00:00] Role

**Interviewer:** Wes, what does your job cover?

**SME:** Everything commercial that isn't turning a wrench. I sell new preventive maintenance agreements, I renew the ones we have, I price commercial repairs and rooftop unit replacements, and I give Trevor the list of PM visits each month. I report to Glen. I've been here since 2012. Before that I was in sales for a supply house.

**Interviewer:** How many agreements do you manage?

**SME:** About 90 right now. Mostly offices, retail strips, a bunch of churches, two private schools. Brookfield Pike Properties is the biggest, 14 buildings on one agreement. Average agreement is around $4,550 a year, and all of them together are about $410,000. They're one-year agreements, 2 to 4 visits a year, so about 290 visits.

**Interviewer:** How do you split your time?

**SME:** Roughly, sales and visiting customers is the biggest piece, then pricing and quotes, then renewals and the agreement sheet. In the role matrix all of that is under sales, about 70%, plus about 25% customer service, because the property managers call me directly. It changes with the season. In spring everybody wants an RTU quote before summer.

## [03:00] The agreement sheet

**Interviewer:** Where do the agreements live?

**SME:** In my spreadsheet, Commercial Agreements. One row per agreement: customer, buildings, visits per year, price, start date, end date, and a notes column for renewal stuff. The signed paper agreements are in a binder in my office. Glen signs anything over $10,000.

**Interviewer:** How does the renewal process work?

**SME:** On paper, the procedure says I filter the sheet on the first business day of the month for end dates 90 days out, send a renewal letter with the new price 90 days before the end, then call at 60 and at 30. That's what we wrote in 2022.

**Interviewer:** And in practice?

**SME:** Honestly, in practice it's not every month and it's not 90 days. I get to renewals when I notice them in the sheet, usually when I'm in there for something else. Most of the time I'm calling somebody in the last month before the end date, and sometimes it's after it already ended. I don't send the letters anymore, I just call or email the property manager with the new price. The 60 and 30 day calls, I don't do those as a rule.

**Interviewer:** What happens when one ends before you renew it?

**SME:** It just lapses. Nothing in the sheet tells me. Usually I find out because Trevor doesn't see it on my visit list, or the customer calls and says we were supposed to be there in May. This year 11 of the 90 went past their end date before I renewed. I got 7 of those back, a few weeks or a couple of months late, and we missed a visit on some of them. Four we lost. One went with a competitor who got there first, the others just stopped.

**Interviewer:** Do you know what those four were worth?

**SME:** Roughly $18,000 a year together. The late ones, we lost some visits we'd have billed, I'm not sure exactly how much.

## [07:00] An example renewal

**Interviewer:** Walk me through a renewal that went well.

**SME:** Harlan Street Church, CA-2025-061. Four RTUs, two visits a year, $3,200, end date June 30th. I called the facilities guy on June 24th, gave him $3,350 for the new year, he signed the same week. That one worked, but it was six days before the end. By the procedure I'd have sent the letter in April.

**Interviewer:** And one that didn't?

**SME:** There was an office building in Gahanna, two units, about $2,800 a year. Ended in March. I didn't call until May, after the property manager emailed asking why we hadn't been out for the spring visit. By then they had a quote from somebody else. We didn't get it back.

## [09:30] Pricing

**Interviewer:** How do you price commercial work?

**SME:** From my pricing sheet, Commercial Pricing 2026. It has our labor rates by visit type, the markup by part category, and RTU pricing by tonnage, crane cost, curb adapters, that kind of thing. I built it over the years. It's on my laptop.

**Interviewer:** Does anyone else use it?

**SME:** No. Dana asks me for the markup when she has a commercial part that's not on the list. Glen knows the general numbers but not the sheet. Kyle has asked me to put it on the shared drive. I keep meaning to clean it up first, there are a lot of tabs that only make sense to me.

**Interviewer:** What happens when you're out?

**SME:** It waits. I was out two weeks in July for a family thing. Six commercial quotes sat until I got back, and two of those customers went with someone else. One was an RTU job, the other was a repair. I'm guessing that was about $21,000 of work between them. Glen tried to do one himself and called me on vacation to ask about the crane cost.

## [11:30] Selling new agreements

**Interviewer:** How do new agreements come in?

**SME:** Mostly from repair work. A building calls us for a no-cool, we fix it, and I follow up and offer a PM agreement. Some come from property managers who already have buildings with us. I don't do cold calling much. This year I signed nine new agreements, which is about normal.

**Interviewer:** How do you build the price for a new one?

**SME:** I walk the building, count the units, note the tonnage and age, and put it in the pricing sheet. The sheet gives me a price per visit by unit count and type. Then I adjust for drive time and how bad the roof access is. It takes me about an hour per agreement once I've walked it. I write the agreement in Word from my template and Glen signs it if it's over $10,000.

**Interviewer:** Do you raise prices at renewal?

**SME:** Usually 3 to 5%. Harlan Street went from $3,200 to $3,350, that's about 4.7%. Some of the older agreements I haven't raised in a couple of years because I renewed them in a hurry and just kept the price.

## [13:00] Commercial PM visits

**Interviewer:** How do the PM visits get scheduled?

**SME:** At the start of the month I give Trevor the list of agreements with visits due. He books them with the building contacts. The tech does the PM checklist for each unit on the paper PM form, filters, belts, coils, refrigerant, electrical. If he finds a repair, he writes it on the form and I quote it separately.

**Interviewer:** How do the PM forms get back to you?

**SME:** Through Dana, with the tickets. I look at them for the repair notes. If a tech writes "recommend replacing compressor, unit 3" I call the customer with a quote. Some of those turn into RTU jobs. I'm probably missing some, because I only see the forms when I go through the pile.

## [14:30] Comfort Club

**Interviewer:** Do you have anything to do with the Comfort Club?

**SME:** Not really. That's the residential plan, $189 a year for two tune-ups. Jolene runs it with Marisol. I know they spend a lot of time on the phone in March and September booking tune-ups, it's 640 members. I think it takes them two or three calls per member to get somebody booked. I'm not sure what happens with the ones they don't reach.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything shared between the commercial and residential agreements?

**SME:** Not really. Different sheets, different people. The only thing in common is they both end up on Trevor's board.

## [16:00] Overlaps and wrap-up

**Interviewer:** You sell, price and renew. Does doing all three help?

**SME:** It helps that I know the customers. When I price a repair I know what their agreement is and who to call. What doesn't help is that it's all me. If I'm in a busy quote week, renewals slip. If I'm out, everything slips. I'd say renewals are what gets pushed when I'm busy, because nothing forces them.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing?

**SME:** Something that tells me an agreement is coming up for renewal without me having to go look. And getting the pricing sheet in shape so Glen or Kyle could use it. That second one is on me.

**Interviewer:** Anything else?

**SME:** Commercial is the part of the business that could grow the most. Property managers talk to each other. When we do a good job at one building, they give us another. When we miss a renewal, they notice.

**Interviewer:** Thanks, Wes.
