# Inventory counting — simulated interview

**Fictional disclosure:** This is a simulated, text-only interview for the fictional Laurel Run Trailer Works, Inc.; no actual interview occurred.  
**Interviewee:** Devon Kersey, Materials and Purchasing Manager  
**Snapshot:** 2026-10-31  
**Topic:** Weekly inventory counts, review, and related material handoffs  
**Intended duration:** Approximately 20 minutes

## [00:00]

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Devon. Could you start by describing what inventory counting is meant to give you in the ordinary course of a week?

**SME:** It gives me a physical check on selected stock we use frequently. Cora Whitlock, our Receiving and Inventory Clerk, does the count. I own the inventory and purchasing records and review what we have and what is coming. The working stock list is an Excel sheet on the shared office drive. I want to know what is actually here, in the unit we use for that item, before I weigh it against an open order or what we are likely to need.

**Interviewer:** Where does your responsibility end and Cora's begin?

**SME:** Cora does the weekly hand count and updates the stock sheet. I review the count, including any differences, and use the updated on-hand information when deciding order quantities. Cora reports to me, as does Jalen Sorrell, our Buyer. I report to Owen Bellamy, the Plant Manager. The count is not the same thing as receiving, although Cora also checks incoming goods against purchase orders.

**Interviewer:** What is the regular trigger, and how much of the inventory does it cover?

**SME:** The routine is weekly. Cora counts six selected, frequently used SKU categories, not every item in the building. The estimate for counting and interpreting those selected lines is about an hour and a half in a working week, with the routine running over roughly 50 working weeks in the year. That's a workload estimate, not a time study for every count. It doesn't tell you the time for a full inventory.

**Interviewer:** Who chooses the six, and is it always the same selection?

**SME:** I'm not sure of the exact selection rule or whether the same six are counted every week. What I can point to is the October 30 sample: it shows six selected lines on that date. It doesn't provide all the other weekly rounds. I would not use that one sample to say we've physically verified the entire stock list.

**Interviewer:** If I asked for a full inventory value at the snapshot, could this count give me that?

**SME:** No. I'm not sure what the total value of all inventory was. The sample is an on-hand example, not a complete ledger, valuation, or reconciliation to the general ledger. The selected materials catalog is not a complete bill of materials either. Those limits matter when someone is trying to make a larger claim from a small set of lines.

## [04:00]

**Interviewer:** Walk me through the count itself, from Cora arriving at the selected material to the point at which you review it.

**SME:** She hand-counts the selected stock and writes the physical counts on paper first. Each physical quantity is compared with the quantity on the Excel stock sheet before the count. The variance is physical count less that pre-count sheet quantity. Cora updates the Excel sheet with the physical count, and the paper count is kept with the inventory records. I review the count and any difference.

**Interviewer:** What do you need on that count record to make the comparison understandable later?

**SME:** The date, item, unit, quantity on the sheet before the count, physical count, variance, and updated balance. If you only see the updated balance, you can't tell whether it agreed with the sheet before the count. The before and after are different facts, even when they happen to be the same number. The sample identifies Cora as counter and me as reviewer; it doesn't supply a complete history of all count activity.

**Interviewer:** You emphasized the unit. What can go wrong in the discussion if we leave it out?

**SME:** We could talk past each other. Axles are counted as each, rectangular steel tube in lengths, mounted wheels as assemblies, and floor screws in boxes. For SKU-0012 a box contains 250 screws. A difference of one box is not a difference of one loose screw. The item identification matters as well: two axle lines are not interchangeable just because both are axles.

**Interviewer:** Is QuickBooks where the shop checks the physical quantity?

**SME:** No. QuickBooks Desktop Enterprise is used for supplier bills; the Excel stock sheet is the working shop-floor count. The paper is the first count record, and the sheet carries the updated quantity. Purchasing and inventory also use Excel lists for suppliers and purchase orders. Those are distinct from the accounting record, so I have to be clear about which information I'm looking at and what it represents.

**Interviewer:** Say the physical count differs from the sheet. Does the record tell you why, or specify an automatic approval threshold?

**SME:** It gives me the difference to review; the count alone doesn't establish its cause. I'm not sure of any documented threshold or a standard recount rule from the records provided. I wouldn't turn a negative variance into a claim about a particular missing transaction or a person. The physical figure is what Cora updates on the sheet, and the earlier sheet figure and variance remain important context for my review.

**Interviewer:** And an open purchase order wouldn't be counted among those physical pieces?

**SME:** Right. Please keep expected receipts separate from on-hand stock. An order that has not arrived isn't here for Cora to count. [Pause.] If someone treats it as stock already in the building, that confuses the purchasing decision and what the floor can actually use. I'm looking at both things, but I don't want them described as the same thing.

## [08:00]

**Interviewer:** Let's use the October 30 sample. What can you actually tell from the axle lines?

**SME:** Cora counted and I reviewed those selected lines. SKU-0001, the 3,500-pound electric-braked spring axle assembly, showed 21 each on the sheet before the count and 21 physically, so the variance was zero and the sheet after was 21. SKU-0002, another 3,500-pound electric-braked spring axle, was 34 each before and physically, and 34 after. Those are separate items with different specifications.

**Interviewer:** How does the third axle compare?

**SME:** SKU-0003 is a 5,200-pound electric-braked spring axle. The pre-count sheet showed 27 each; the physical count was 26. Physical less the earlier sheet number is minus one, and the updated balance was 26. That is an example of the review we just discussed. I'm not sure why that one differed, and the sample doesn't tell us that the difference was investigated or resolved beyond the recorded update and review.

**Interviewer:** What about the other units on that same count?

**SME:** SKU-0004 rectangular steel tube was 73 lengths before and physically, with 73 after. SKU-0005 mounted 15-inch tire and wheel was 110 assemblies before and physically, with 110 after. SKU-0012 floor screws were 18 boxes on the sheet before, 17 physically, and 17 after; the variance was minus one box. Cora's record makes the unit explicit so I don't have to guess whether we mean pieces, lengths, or boxes.

**Interviewer:** Is that screw-box difference related to the earlier screw shortage?

**SME:** I'm not sure. The shortage file has an event for SKU-0012 on September 16, while the count is from October 30. That does not establish a causal connection between them or tell me what happened to every box between those dates. It would be easy to tell a story that sounds tidy, but those records alone don't support it.

**Interviewer:** How detailed are the item specifications when you are considering one of those axle lines?

**SME:** The material catalog distinguishes them. For example, SKU-0001 includes electric brakes, both drums and hubs; springs and hangers are separate. SKU-0003 has a different rating and hub pattern. I would use the item and specification, not just a vague description like “axle.” The catalog covers selected current supplier-of-record items, not every possible material we buy.

## [12:00]

**Interviewer:** Once the sheet reflects the count, how does it become a purchasing decision?

**SME:** I compare the updated on-hand quantities with open purchase orders and likely near-term requirements. Then I use those inputs and my experience to decide order quantities. On-hand is one part of the picture, expected receipts are another, and likely requirements are another. I'm not sure of a documented formula, reorder point, or comprehensive forecast in the supplied records, so I wouldn't present my judgment as one.

**Interviewer:** Who does what when you decide a purchase is needed?

**SME:** Jalen prepares the purchase order. I approve the PO and the quantity; Jalen assigns the PO number and emails it to the supplier. My approval is part of that purchase step. A proposed order isn't an on-hand balance, and approving it doesn't mean it has been delivered. Those may seem like small distinctions, but they make a difference when someone asks what we can use now.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me a documented purchase example without treating it as the entire purchasing history?

**SME:** PO-2026-0237 was ordered on October 8, 2026. All 50 SKU-0001 axle assemblies were received from Ridge Hollow Components LLC on October 15. The price was $276 each, delivered excluding sales tax. That's one monthly release, not a full purchasing ledger. By the October 30 count, the record shows 21 SKU-0001 on hand; it doesn't give a complete movement trail explaining every change between receipt and count.

**Interviewer:** What happens at the receiving bay, and who gets involved if the delivery doesn't match?

**SME:** Cora counts the goods against the PO and signs the packing slip. If there is damage or a short receipt, she sends that to Jalen before updating the stock sheet. The bill packet also matters: Elena Corwin, the Accounting Clerk, matches the supplier bill, signed packing slip, and PO. I approve exceptions. If a packet is missing something or doesn't match, it comes to me for exception approval before entry; if I'm away, it comes back to me when I'm available.

**Interviewer:** Are you saying that happened on the Ridge Hollow release?

**SME:** No. That release is documented as received in full. I was describing the ordinary exception route, not claiming there was damage, a short receipt, or a mismatched bill on that PO. I'm not sure whether any exception occurred on that release, and I don't have a reason in the count sample to assert one.

**Interviewer:** How does information from your area reach the people sequencing the floor?

**SME:** Jonah Sutter, our Production Scheduler, uses stock notes and supervisor input when sequencing work. Theo Lowry and Avery Carver report material exceptions from fabrication and assembly. If there's a material exception, tell me and make sure Jonah gets the floor report for the schedule. Jonah owns the schedule; I own the inventory and purchasing records. I'm not sure of a documented timing requirement for every such handoff.

## [16:00]

**Interviewer:** When material isn't available, what does the floor do while that is being worked through?

**SME:** Hold the work that needs the missing material and use other work when available. That is not necessarily a whole-plant stop. The shortage records show four separate events in the reporting year. In each record, three workers were held for three hours before reassignment to other work. Those records are examples of interruptions, not a complete inventory movement history.

**Interviewer:** Could you put one of those examples in context without assuming what caused it?

**SME:** On September 16, 2026, the recorded shortage was SKU-0012, the floor-screw box. Three workers were held for three hours each, then reassigned to other work; that's nine staff-hours held in that event. The record says no substitution and no safety compromise. It doesn't say that everyone in the plant stopped, and I'm not sure why the shortage occurred or what replenishment action followed that particular event.

**Interviewer:** What were the other recorded items?

**SME:** Rectangular steel tube, SKU-0004, on December 3, 2025; the mounted 15-inch tire and wheel, SKU-0005, on March 18, 2026; and painted aluminum sheet, SKU-0007, on June 17, 2026. Each has the same recorded hold and reassignment pattern I described. I wouldn't infer that the October count figures were the quantities on hand at the earlier events, or that the same cause applied to each.

**Interviewer:** If something similar is on the shelf, can purchasing or the supervisor simply authorize its use instead?

**SME:** No. Felix Harrow, our Manufacturing Engineer, and Iris Calder, our Quality and Warranty Lead, must confirm fit and the required specifications before material is substituted. Depending on the item, dimensions, capacity, electrical details, and bolt pattern matter. An axle's rating alone doesn't establish a trailer's payload; check the completed trailer's certification and load labels. A delivery that hasn't arrived or an assumed substitute doesn't bypass that check.

**Interviewer:** Thinking about the day-to-day handoffs, where do you have to be especially careful when explaining the numbers?

**SME:** When I move from Cora's paper count to the updated sheet and then consider open POs and the floor's likely needs, I need to keep the references straight. The earlier sheet number is not the physical count if there's a difference. An incoming order is not an item on the shelf. And a stock note is not a guarantee that every future requirement is covered. The records are useful, but they answer different questions.

**Interviewer:** What would you want an interviewer not to conclude from this material?

**SME:** Don't assume the sample proves the total inventory value, the cause of the differences, or a plant-wide interruption. I'm also not sure of an overall count-accuracy measure, the time for every handoff, or a complete history of shortages from these files. A missing measure isn't zero. We have the weekly workload estimate and these specific examples; for the other questions, I'd need evidence beyond what's here.

**Interviewer:** Thank you, Devon. That's helpful.

*End of simulated interview, at approximately 20 minutes.*
