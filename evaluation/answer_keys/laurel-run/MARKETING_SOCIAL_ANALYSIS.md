# Laurel Run marketing and social-media analysis — evaluator only, do not share with the analysis agent

**Fictional case.** Founded in 1988, Laurel Run is a family-owned Pennsylvania manufacturer with 50 employees and approximately 80 independent dealers. Its age and family ownership do not establish weak digital marketing. Activity below covers November 1, 2025–October 31, 2026; follower counts are snapshots at October 31. All Laurel Run facts and results are invented. [1] [2]

## Channel assessment

**Facebook.** The company published **18 posts in 10 of 12 months** and had **248 followers**. Of 13 qualifying product or availability inquiry threads, all received an initial reply within two business days; 11 received dealer contact information, and five of those were forwarded with permission. Facebook had more recorded posts and inquiries than Instagram, but unknown reach and different activity levels prevent an effectiveness ranking. [2] [3]

**Instagram.** The company published **seven posts in six months** and had **73 followers**. All seven posts reused topics and photos that appeared on Facebook in the same month. All four qualifying inquiry threads received timely initial replies and dealer contact information; one was forwarded with permission. Reuse can be practical for a small team, but the records do not show whether the channel reached a different audience or influenced purchases. Do not add the two follower counts as distinct people. [2] [3]

**TikTok.** There was **no company account and no posting**. This is not proof of a missed sale or a reason the company must open an account. First consider whether a relevant audience and a safe, sustainable video workflow justify a pilot; an approved short video could also be tested on existing channels. [2] [3]

**Website and dealers.** The website supplies company and product information; Keira prepares product sheets and dealer handouts. Keep specifications consistent and provide current dealer contact information. Check whether a dealer-finding feature exists before proposing one. Dealers set retail prices and handle purchases; these materials should complement dealer relationships. [1] [2] NIST's guidance discusses networking alongside digital outreach, without quantifying sales effects. [9]

## Ownership and what the evidence means

Keira Morrow, Marketing and Dealer Support Coordinator, allocates about 70% of her time to marketing. She maintains the Excel calendar and recap, checks accounts, and uses approved information and photos. Tessa Bellamy approves material; Keira publishes and retains the approval. Posting was irregular, but the gaps are unexplained. [2] [4]

Across both channels, all **17 qualifying inbound threads** received an initial reply within two business days. **Fifteen** received dealer contact information; **six consented forwards are a subset of those 15**, not additional inquiries. The other two sought product information only. Threads need not represent distinct people. Dealer contact is not a confirmed visit or purchase; a dealer's retail sale is not Laurel Run's wholesale revenue. [2] [3]

The recap does not systematically record organic reach, impressions, engagement, video views, website traffic sources, link clicks, dealer follow-through, retail conversion, or social-attributed manufacturer orders and revenue. These outcomes are **unknown, not zero**. Likes and followers are not sales, and reach and conversion are separate. [2] [5]

## Practical next steps and staged measurement

1. **Pilot a manageable calendar.** For 90 days, Keira and Tessa could plan one or two Facebook posts per month and one appropriately formatted Instagram reuse per month, adjusting for capacity. Use approved product features, photos or dealer announcements with a dealer-contact call to action. Batch Tessa's approval, retain it, and identify a trained account-checking backup. These are proposals, not benchmarks or existing commitments.
2. **Protect people and claims.** Obtain appropriate employee, customer and dealer permission before filming or publishing. Avoid disclosing personal or confidential dealer information, and do not stage unsafe shop or towing practices. Verify specifications against approved information and the completed unit's labels; avoid unsupported payload, availability and price claims.
3. **Simplify the recap and referral handoff.** Keep an access-controlled monthly template with source links and separate inquiry, dealer-contact and consented-forward counts. Assign a follow-up owner; document consent and minimize retained personal data. Agree with willing dealers on permissible feedback and retention. A referral does not confer marketing consent. Optional AI may assist with drafts from approved, nonconfidential material only; Keira checks facts and Tessa approves. Never use AI for invented claims, safety decisions or automatic outreach.
4. **Measure in stages.** In month one, retain baseline definitions and time the recap. Where available and appropriately configured, record each platform's reach and clicks separately, plus website traffic sources and dealer-contact clicks; mark unavailable data as unknown. During the pilot, log activity and timely replies. For consented referrals, request optional dealer feedback and distinguish confirmed follow-through or retail outcomes from unknown/no feedback. At 90 days, Keira and Tessa review effort, results and coverage with dealers, then continue, revise or stop. A confirmed purchase still does not prove a post caused it or establish attributable manufacturer revenue. [2] [3] [5]

**Evaluator accounting.** The 12 recap entries total **540 minutes**, or nine hours, excluding posts, approvals, replies, forwards and handouts. The existing **15-issue key already includes social-related I15**; this is not another issue. If a simpler recap took an assumed 15 minutes monthly, it would take three hours annually. Six hours at an assumed **$32 per staff-hour** gives a **$192 illustrative annual net opportunity**, not observed savings or an additive issue. The counterfactual is untested, excludes implementation costs and need not reduce cash payroll. No lost sales or reach are monetized. [3] [6]

## External context, not a Laurel Run benchmark

An agency survey of **114 U.S. manufacturing respondents** found 70% represented firms with fewer than 100 employees, while 10% reported no digital-marketing investment. Recruitment and representativeness are not established; no investment does not mean no website or social accounts. [7] Separately, **95% of 104 manufacturing marketers worldwide** in a content-marketing survey reported organic social distribution; half worked at firms with at least 1,000 employees. This is not a target or representative small-trailer benchmark.
