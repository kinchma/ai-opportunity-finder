Lean is a continuous-improvement approach that removes activities that add no value for the customer. The MEP National Network describes it as the foundation for automation and for retaining a workforce, because operational efficiency comes first. MEP centers exist in every state and work with small and mid-sized manufacturers.

Value stream mapping is usually the first step: a high-level view of material and information flow for one product family that exposes waste and shows where to focus. See the separate value-stream-mapping note.

5S (sort, set in order, shine, standardize, sustain) organizes a work area so unnecessary items are removed, needed tools are visible and their place is obvious. It improves safety and makes maintenance easier, and it is often the first improvement a small shop can do without spending money.

Quick changeover (setup reduction) shortens the time between the last good piece of one run and the first good piece of the next, which lets a shop run smaller batches and respond faster to custom orders.

Cellular or flow manufacturing arranges steps and equipment so work moves continuously with balanced workloads; benefits include higher productivity, shorter lead times, lower inventory and faster resolution of quality problems.

Kanban is a pull system: replenishment is triggered by actual customer demand rather than forecasts, which lowers inventory and stock-outs at the same time.

Six Sigma uses statistical quality methods to remove causes of defects and reduce variation. Toyota Kata builds a habit of improvement through a repeated improvement routine and a coaching routine.

For a small manufacturer the usual improvement data (defect logs, rework, scrap, setup times, schedule adherence, on-time delivery) is scattered across paper check sheets, spreadsheets and email. Collecting it in one place and counting it in code is a prerequisite for any AI-assisted analysis.
