Bookkeeping should stay current so the owner always knows where the business stands. The SBA's basic advice is to keep books up to date, understand the basic financial statements, and have one person clearly responsible for receivables, payables, available cash, bank reconciliation and payroll.

A balance sheet is a snapshot of what the business owns and owes; use it to track capital and project cash flow. Segmenting results (for example online sales versus in-person sales, or one product line versus another) shows which parts of the business make money.

Use a simple cost-benefit analysis before spending: estimate the yearly profit a change would add and compare it with its one-time and ongoing costs. The SBA example is a restaurant weighing outdoor seating that adds about $5,000 a year in profit against about $3,000 in permits and equipment.

Choose an accounting method and keep to it. Accrual accounting records a sale when it is completed and gives an immediate picture of performance, but it is more complex and can make cash look better than it is. Cash accounting records a sale when the money arrives, which is simpler and shows real cash flow, but has little predictive value. GAAP standardizes accrual reporting; private companies are not required to follow it.

Outside help comes in three forms: a CPA (more expensive, tailored advice and tax work), a bookkeeper (lower cost, less formal training) or an online bookkeeping service. Many small businesses combine a bookkeeper or software for day-to-day entries with a CPA at year end.

Repetitive, rule-based finance tasks in a small business include entering invoices and receipts, matching payments to invoices, reconciling the bank account monthly, chasing overdue receivables and producing the monthly statements. These are the finance tasks most often done by hand in small companies.
