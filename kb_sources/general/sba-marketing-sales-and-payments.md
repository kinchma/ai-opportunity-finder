A marketing plan turns strategy into actions and should line up with the business plan. Its sections are: target market; competitive advantage; sales plan; marketing and sales goals; an action plan covering channels, pricing, promotions and post-sale support; budget; measurement and updates; and the operations that shape the customer's experience.

Measure return on investment by comparing marketing cost with the revenue it brings in, and update the plan at least once a year. The SBA example is a shop that targets anyone with a car, competes on service and affordability, sets a monthly new-customer goal, advertises locally and schedules an ROI review.

Lead handling is where small businesses lose sales: inquiries arrive by web form, phone, email, walk-in and trade shows, and often nobody owns the follow-up. Capturing every lead in one place with a status and a next action is the basic control.

Accepting payments: card payments need a merchant account or processor, carry per-transaction fees, and should use EMV chip readers to limit fraud. Checks need a business bank account and a clear acceptance policy; bounced-check recovery rules vary by state. Cash needs secure storage, and cash receipts over $10,000 from one buyer must be reported to the IRS on Form 8300. Online payments need a payment service and a shopping cart that handles totals, taxes and shipping.

Customer follow-up after the sale (order status, warranty questions, reorders, reviews) is routine, repetitive and usually done by the same people who sell, so it competes with new business for their time.
