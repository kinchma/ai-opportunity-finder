The IRS gives five reasons to keep business records: to monitor progress (what sells, what needs changing), to prepare income statements and balance sheets for the bank and management, to identify the source of each receipt (business versus personal, taxable versus not), to track deductible expenses that are otherwise forgotten at tax time, and to prepare returns and support the income, expenses and credits reported.

The law does not require a particular bookkeeping system; any system that clearly shows income and expenses is acceptable. Keep a separate complete set of records for each business. A corporation should also keep minutes of board meetings.

Supporting documents by type: gross receipts (cash register tapes, bank deposit slips, receipt books, invoices, card slips, Forms 1099); inventory (canceled checks, receipts, card slips, invoices); expenses (canceled checks, register tapes, account statements, card slips, invoices, petty cash slips); assets (purchase and sales invoices, closing statements, canceled checks, plus the acquisition date and price, improvements, depreciation taken, how the asset was used and its disposal). Proof of payment alone does not establish a deduction; keep the document that shows the cost was incurred.

Day-to-day practice: record transactions daily when possible; treat the business checkbook as the main source of the books; keep business and personal accounts separate; deposit all receipts into the business account and pay by check or electronic transfer rather than cash; reconcile the bank account every month and post service charges and other unrecorded items.

Single-entry bookkeeping (a daily summary of cash receipts plus monthly summaries of receipts and disbursements) is the simplest system for a small business. Double-entry bookkeeping posts every transaction as a debit and a credit to journals and ledgers. Computerized systems must produce legible records that reconcile to the books and the return, and the business must keep a description of the system's processing and controls.

Electronic storage systems must index, store, preserve, retrieve and reproduce records legibly and give the IRS access to complete, accurate data (Revenue Procedure 97-22). Paper copies may be destroyed only after the electronic system is tested.

How long to keep records: generally until the period of limitations for the return expires; 3 years in the usual case; 6 years if income was under-reported by more than 25%; 7 years for worthless securities or bad-debt deductions; no limit for a fraudulent return or no return; employment tax records at least 4 years; asset records until the limitations period expires for the year the asset is disposed of.

Document extraction and matching (invoice to purchase order to payment), monthly reconciliation and record retention are the parts of this that are routine, repetitive and rule-governed, which makes them candidates for assisted automation with a person approving exceptions.
