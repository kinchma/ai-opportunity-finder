A business picks a tax year (calendar, fiscal or a short year for a partial period) and keeps it. State and local obligations depend on location and legal structure; the most common are income and employment taxes. Corporations are taxed separately from their owners, while sole proprietors report business income on their personal returns.

Employment taxes can include workers' compensation, unemployment insurance and temporary disability insurance, plus withheld employee income tax. Deposits and filings run on quarterly and annual cycles, which is why payroll is usually outsourced or run from software with a calendar.

Staying compliant means keeping internal records that prove it: corporations need meetings, minutes, bylaws and stock records; LLCs should keep an updated operating agreement, record membership transfers and hold annual meetings; other structures have few formal requirements, but documenting key decisions is recommended.

Ongoing state filings include annual reports or biennial statements (fees can exceed $300), franchise taxes, initial reports and articles of amendment. Federal requirements include taxes, Affordable Care Act reporting for businesses with 50 or more employees, and keeping federal licenses current. Marketing, copyright, workplace posters, OSHA and ADA rules apply as well.

Licenses and permits have renewal cycles that vary by industry and location (health certificates for restaurants, sales permits for regulated goods, and federal agencies such as USDA, TTB, FAA, ATF, Fish and Wildlife, NOAA and FCC for specific trades). A renewal calendar with owner, due date and status is a simple control most small businesses lack.

Names and marks: an entity name is registered with the state, a trademark gives national protection (check the USPTO database first), a DBA usually must be registered but gives no legal protection by itself, and domain names must be renewed on time.
