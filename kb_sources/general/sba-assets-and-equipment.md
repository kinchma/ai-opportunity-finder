Assets fall into three groups: tangible (buildings, vehicles, equipment; consumables such as paper usually are not counted), intangible (reputation, brand, partner networks) and intellectual property (trademarks, patents, logos, websites, domains, software).

Leasing suits equipment needed quickly or too expensive to buy. Pros: lower upfront cost, short-term testing, sometimes free maintenance and tax-deductible payments. Cons: higher lifetime cost, possibly expensive replacement, and depreciation cannot be deducted. An operating lease works like a rental and stays off the balance sheet; a capital lease works like a loan, appears on the balance sheet and allows depreciation and interest deductions but shifts maintenance, risk and tax obligations to the business. Check buyout options, lease length and early-termination penalties.

Buying suits long-term use when cash or credit is available. Pros: depreciation deductions, usually lower lifetime cost, and a balance-sheet asset. Cons: more cash upfront, less chance to test and full maintenance liability. Loans and lines of credit spread the cost but add fees and interest.

Government surplus (USA.gov auctions, military surplus, U.S. Marshals asset forfeiture, Treasury and IRS auctions) sells equipment and property, usually as-is, at or below market prices.

For a small company the practical record is an asset register: what was bought, when, for how much, where it is, who maintains it, its lease or loan terms and renewal dates. It is often a spreadsheet, or missing.
