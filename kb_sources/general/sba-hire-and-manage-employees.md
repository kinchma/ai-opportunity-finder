Setting up payroll follows a fixed sequence: get an Employer Identification Number; check whether state or local tax IDs are needed; decide whether each worker is an employee or an independent contractor; collect a Form W-4 from each employee; set pay periods; write holiday, vacation and leave rules; choose an in-house or outside payroll service; name the person who manages payroll; learn which records must be kept and for how long; and file payroll taxes quarterly and annually.

Misclassifying an employee as a contractor is a common and costly mistake: it can mean back taxes, penalties, benefits and wage reimbursement under the Fair Labor Standards Act. Keep employment tax records for at least four years.

Required benefits include the employer's share of Social Security taxes, workers' compensation insurance, unemployment insurance (rules vary by state) and, in a few states, disability insurance. Health plans, retirement plans, COBRA continuation and extra leave are optional, but federal rules apply once they are offered.

The SBA's worked example is an auto repair shop: it writes job descriptions, pays at or above the state minimum wage, keeps Forms W-4 and I-9 on file, reports new hires to the state within 20 days and uses an online payroll service. The pattern for most small businesses is the same: a short checklist of forms and deadlines plus an outside payroll service.

Federal and state labor laws also cover veterans, foreign workers, household employees, child labor, people with disabilities, terminations and layoffs. Small businesses rarely have an HR person, so onboarding paperwork, policy acknowledgements and training records tend to be handled by the owner or an office manager and are a frequent gap.
